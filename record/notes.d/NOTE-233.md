# NOTE-233: TinyStories

## Contribution

Before this, "small models cannot produce coherent text" was a fact with two
available explanations and no way to choose between them: natural language is
intrinsically too hard below some scale, or web-scale corpora ask a small
model to learn English and an encyclopaedia simultaneously. This paper builds
a corpus that removes the encyclopaedia and keeps the English, and the small
models start speaking. That converts a scale claim into a **data** claim.

It also introduced, as instrumentation, grading generated text with a large
model on separate axes — what the field later called model-as-a-judge — on
the argument that the benchmarks available in 2023 could not score free
generation at all.

## Key insight

**Coherence was never the expensive part; breadth was.** A model with an
embedding dimension of 256 has enough capacity for grammar, discourse and
simple reasoning, and does not have enough for the factual mass of the Pile.
Train it on a corpus whose vocabulary and world are a three-year-old's and
what was missing turns out not to have been linguistic competence.

The corollary is the one that made this a research instrument rather than a
curiosity: you can now have a *model organism* — something small enough to
interpret and real enough to behave like the thing you care about.

## Assumptions

- **The dataset is generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4**, so the target
  distribution is a commercial model's idea of a children's story. Everything
  downstream inherits whatever that is.
- **Diversity has to be engineered in.** Prompting for stories at high
  temperature yields a repetitive corpus; the mechanism here is three words
  (a noun, a verb, an adjective) drawn at random from a ~1,500-word vocabulary
  and required to appear, plus a random subset of features.
- GPT-Neo architecture, window size 256, context 512, the GPT-Neo tokenizer
  truncated to its top 10K tokens.
- **Evaluation is a language model's judgement**, validated against the
  authors' reading rather than against human raters at scale.

## Key results

- Models **below 10M parameters** (embedding dimension 256) produce fluent,
  consistent, multi-paragraph stories with near-perfect grammar.
- A **one-transformer-block** model at 21M produces syntactically correct
  English but loses the plot — the one-layer case is where the separation
  between grammar and coherence is starkest.
- Reasoning of the disjunction-elimination kind appears at **2.5M**.
- **Factual knowledge tracks the embedding dimension; context-tracking and
  instruction-following track the number of layers.** Grammar is what a
  shallow model gets right first.
- Training runs in **under a day on a single GPU**, which is what made the
  hyperparameter sweeps in the paper possible at all.
- One- and two-layer models show attention heads separating cleanly into
  local and semantic, and MLP neurons firing on tokens with a shared role.

## Claims

**Well supported:** that corpus breadth rather than linguistic complexity is
what small models were failing on. The intervention is on the data, the
architecture is held, and the effect is large enough not to need statistics.

**Well supported and often overstated in citation:** the depth/width
separation. It is a real sweep across 1–8 layers and several embedding
dimensions, and it is a sweep over *one* architecture family on *one* corpus
with GPT-Eval as the instrument.

**Anticipated and tested, not merely asserted:** that the models are not
memorizing templates. Word and n-gram overlap against the training set plus
out-of-distribution prompts; the Figure 1 prompt was verified to have no
6-gram overlap.

**Assumed rather than shown:** that the generation procedure produced a
diverse corpus. The paper argues the mechanism should work and does not
measure the result. [LIT-485](../literature.d/LIT-485.md) measured it, and 59.38% of the
stories begin "once upon a time".

**A claim about the models, not the data:** that small models are more
interpretable. Demonstrated by inspection of one- and two-layer models rather
than by a metric.

## Method

Generate a synthetic corpus of short stories under a vocabulary constraint,
train GPT-Neo models from ~1M to ~35M parameters and 1 to 8 layers on it, and
grade completions with GPT-4 on separate axes. Sweep depth against embedding
dimension, head count against width, and compute against model size.

## Bearing on the record

It is the trunk of the `tiny-models` line, which the record was eight
practices into without holding.

**It does not satisfy [SOTA-125](../practices.d/SOTA-125.md)'s promotion condition**, and the reason is
worth stating rather than leaving to inference. That practice trades depth
against **MLP width** in a hybrid at 90M and asks for a hybrid from another
group or another 90M-class ablation. This sweeps depth against **embedding
dimension** in pure-attention GPT-Neo at 1–35M and has no SSM state
dimension to trade against at all. Two findings that share the words "depth"
and "width" and are not the same measurement.

## Limitations

**One corpus, and a strange one.** Everything is about a distribution
designed to be simple. Whether the depth/width separation or the emergence
scales transfer to a corpus with real factual mass is exactly what the paper
removed in order to get its result.

**The judge is a language model**, on a task where the generating and grading
distributions are both GPT-family.

**"Below 10M parameters" excludes embeddings**, which is not stated in the
headline and is a live source of confusion — `LIT-485` puts
TinyStories-33M at 68M all-in and recommends the convention be changed.

**Interpretability is argued by example.** Clear attention patterns in a
one-layer model are shown, not quantified against a baseline.

## Open questions

- Does the corpus-breadth explanation have a quantitative form? The paper
  establishes the direction and puts no number on how much breadth costs how
  much capacity.
- What is the smallest model that speaks, counted honestly? The paper's own
  answer excludes embeddings, and `LIT-485` reopens the question under a
  stricter count.
