I filed `2312.02696` as `LIT-720`, with a practice and a theory behind it. It was
already `LIT-714`, with a better practice and the theory's claim already inside it.
`arxiv` is declared unique on `LIT`, so the second filing turned `main` red:

> `luria.yaml`: `schemes.LIT.field_groups.source` declares `arxiv` unique, and
> `2312.02696` is held by [LIT-714](../../record/literature.d/LIT-714.md), [LIT-720](../../record/literature.d/LIT-720.md) — one value cannot identify 2
> documents; retire all but one, naming the survivor in `superseded_by:`

Everything below is the reconstruction, because the entry that is worth writing is
not "a duplicate happened" but "which checks were run, and why every one of them
passed".

## The checks that passed

Before starting I measured the gap, deliberately, because the previous two units
had paid off by doing exactly that:

    grep -rn "2312.02696\|EDM2\|EDM-2" record/ --include=*.md
    grep -rln "post-hoc EMA\|magnitude-preserving" record/practices.d record/theory.d record/literature.d

Both came back clean — only curation entries mentioning EDM2 as a *ranking*, and
zero files naming either contribution. I also ran the branch-point check this
record gained a paragraph for an hour earlier:

    git ls-tree -r --name-only origin/main record/ | grep -c tmp   # 0

**All three were correct and all three were blind to the same thing.** The paper
was being filed on another branch at that moment. A branch is invisible to a grep
over the working tree, invisible to a grep over `main`, and invisible to the
temp-code check — which exists to catch codes *already merged* to `main` and
awaiting numbering, not codes that have not landed at all.

So the uniqueness constraint did its job: it is the only check in the system
positioned to catch this, and it caught it at the first moment it could, which is
after both branches merged. That is not a gap in the lint. It is what a
serialization point is for.

## What the duplicate cost, and what it did not

`LIT-714` wins on every axis and it is not close. It was read "in full … appendices
included" as `NOTE-370`; it carries the whole A–G ablation ladder with FIDs
(8.00 → 2.56); it has the finding I did not have, that FID and FD_DINOv2 pick EMA
lengths **13% against 19%** unguided and **2% against 14%** at guidance 1.4, each
rating the other's optimum as poor; and ten documents cite it against my four, all
of which were my own.

`SOTA-428` likewise covers `SOTA-432`, with more: architecture, learning rate,
guidance *and* metric dependence, where mine had the first two. And the theory I
filed as `THEORY-111` is a sentence in its Conditions — "Much of what looks like
learning-rate sensitivity is EMA mismatch" — resting on the same
72%-against-10% bracket I built a whole document on. I even misread which figure
it was (12 in the v1 HTML, 13 in the published version), which `NOTE-370` has
right.

**Two things survive**, and only because they touch documents `SOTA-428` does not
mention: the `SOTA-415` qualification (its "no hyperparameter" justification for
uniform averaging described the scheme rather than the choice) and the `SOTA-408`
note (its extra-epochs charge belongs to its method, not to averaging). Both are
repointed at the live documents and both are unchanged.

## The judgement I got wrong, and it was not the grep

The measurement said: six averaging practices, zero mentions of post-hoc EMA,
therefore a gap worth a unit. That inference was right, and somebody else had
already drawn it — `LIT-714`'s Standing section says "**neither of its two
contributions was anywhere in this record**", which is my sentence, arrived at
independently, hours earlier.

So the failure was not analysis. It was that **I treated a stale worklist as a
claim about the present.** `#342`'s body is a snapshot from 2026-09-24 that says
"21 of 25 unfiled", and by the time I read it eleven of its fourteen Tier B items
were done — several by me. An issue body is not a database view; nothing updates
it. Reading "EDM2, item 9, unfiled" off a day-old table and starting work is the
same class of error as reading a gap sentence out of a note and believing it, which
this journal has now recorded three times in two days. I wrote that finding down
and then made the same mistake with a different artifact.

**The cheap check I skipped**: `grep -l 2312.02696 record/literature.d/*.md` costs
nothing, and I ran the *right* query against the *wrong* moment — before branching
rather than before committing. A pre-commit re-measurement would have caught it,
since `#375` merged while I was reading.

## The retirement, and why not deletion

`CLAUDE.md` says retire by changing status, never by deleting, and the lint's own
message prescribes `superseded_by:`. Both point the same way, and deleting would
also hide the error, which given whose error it is is the wrong bias. Three
`Superseded` shells with `formerly:` aliases keep every stray citation resolving
and leave the record saying out loud that this happened.

The record has deleted duplicates before — 67 of them, after `#138` — so the
precedent exists. The distinction I am drawing: those were a mechanical import
artifact with no reasoning attached, and these are three documents somebody wrote
on purpose and got wrong.
