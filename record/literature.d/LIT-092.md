# LIT-092: Understanding Contrastive Learning Requires Incorporating Inductive Biases

Saunshi et al. (2022) — [ARXIV-2202.14037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.14037)

## Key takeaways

- **A negative result about a class of theory.** Analyses that prove downstream
  guarantees from two things — properties of the augmentations and the value of
  the contrastive loss — **cannot** explain contrastive learning's success, and
  are **provably vacuous** in some settings
- The falsification is direct: across image and text, "different function
  classes and algorithms behave very differently on downstream tasks, **despite
  having the same augmentations and contrastive losses**". If two setups agree
  on everything a bound depends on and disagree on the outcome, the bound is not
  explaining the outcome
- Formalises prior work (Arora et al., Tosh et al., HaoChen et al.) through a
  **transfer function** abstraction — a map from contrastive-loss performance to
  downstream performance — and shows any such map must be uninformative
- **The positive half:** for linear representations, admitting the function
  class's inductive bias into the analysis gives guarantees under *less*
  stringent conditions than the augmentation-only accounts require. The fix is
  not to abandon theory but to include the bias

## Standing in the anthology

Read — [NOTE-070](../notes.d/NOTE-070.md). The document is `Rejected` and the reading does not argue
with that; contrastive representation learning is not a line this anthology
tracks, and the positive result is for linear models.

It is worth having read for the method: **hold fixed everything an explanation
depends on, vary what it ignores, and see whether the outcome follows.** The
record contains many claims of the form "X works because of Y", and this is the
cheapest available test of one.

Its takeaways named the paper's ingredients without its conclusion — a reader
would take "performance bounds" to mean the paper is *about* such bounds rather
than *against* a family of them.
