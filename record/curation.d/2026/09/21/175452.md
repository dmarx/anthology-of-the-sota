**Genesys (`LIT-tmpesppq`) filed from the [#180](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/180) worklist, for a result that
is not the one on the front of the paper.**

The advertised finding is that an LLM agent system discovered language-model
architectures competitive with GPT2 and Mamba2 — 6 of 9 benchmarks — after
proposing, implementing and *actually pretraining* 1,062 designs. That is a
serious experiment and, as far as I can tell, the largest of its kind. It is
also the claim this record does not file.

The claim it does file is in Table 4, and it is about code generation. Same
model, same 100 held-out proposals, two strategies:

| | valid | lines of function body |
|---|---|---|
| unit-by-unit + checker | **92%** | 181 |
| one-shot with retries | **6%** | 49 |

Fifteen times the success rate, and the failures are not the interesting part
— the *successes* of one-shot prompting are trivial. 49 lines where the human
reference library averages 220, and the paper's example of what you get is
"basic ConvNets". A model asked for a whole complex artifact in one go will
hand you something simple enough to be valid.

The mechanism is stated and has nothing to do with architectures. One-shot
generation needs `E[calls] = 1/p` where `p` is the probability that
*everything* is right at once, which collapses as artifacts grow. Generate
unit by unit, freeze what passes a checker, and one joint probability becomes
a product of local ones you pay for separately. So the practice is written at
that level, and the promotion condition asks for the comparison on some other
kind of artifact — by somebody not selling the scaffold.

**The ordering inside the ablation is the part I would keep if I kept one
number.** Removing the symbolic checker costs 62 points of validity. Removing
the unit-wise decomposition costs 19. The decomposition is the clever idea
with the Viterbi proof in the appendix; the checker is a script that runs a
forward pass. The boring half is three times the lever.

Two of the loop's agents — a planner and an observer — moved validity by one
and three points. The paper says they matter qualitatively, which may well be
true and is not what the table measures. That went in the Conditions, because
an agent count is not a mechanism.

**Now the part I spent most of the time on.** The 6/9 claim is *literally
accurate*. I checked all nine columns: six wins, three losses, against the
best seed on each. But it is a maximum over the five best of 1,062 designs,
and the average column tells a different story — best discovered 61.81 against
best seed 61.78, a margin of **0.03**, and the five discovered designs average
**60.17** against their five seeds' **60.77**. On aggregate the search
produced architectures slightly *worse* than the ones it started from.

And the nine benchmarks were selected, per the paper's own Table 16, by
largest standard deviation across the search population and by the best design
beating random by more than 5% — statistics of the search itself. The fitness
function being optimized is average downstream accuracy. So selection and
evaluation share a metric, and the paper says so.

**Which is why this one goes in the ledger as a credit, not a demerit.** Table
16 is published. The selection standard is written down. Every design, every
agent dialogue and every training run is online at `genesys.allen.ai`. The
abstract's headline number is given as "~86 *percentage point* improvement",
which is the correct and less flattering framing of a 15× ratio — most papers
would have written "15×". A paper that hands you the instrument to check its
weakest claim has done something the last week of reading suggests is rare.

`DP-010`'s v2 instruction — look for the underreported sentence too — applies
here in an unusual form. Nothing is underreported. Both results are in the
abstract, in the right order of confidence, with the right words. What varies
is only which one a reader will quote, and that is not the authors' doing.
I am not counting this toward `DP-010`'s tally for that reason; the pattern
that principle tracks is compression that is not selective for evidence, and
here the compression is fine and the selection happens downstream, in us.

**What I did not do is invent a practice out of the criticism.** "Evaluate a
search on tasks held out of its fitness function" is a real gap — I checked,
and the record has nothing on it — but Genesys is an *instance* of the
problem, not a measurement of it. Filing it with Genesys as `source:` would
be citing a paper for something it does not say, which is the thing the note
template exists to prevent. It went to [#241](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/241) instead, with the measurement
that would close it: the checkpoints are public, so somebody could score those
five designs on held-out tasks in one evaluation run and turn the instance
into evidence.

**And one pass-over with a reason.** `2504.06209`, *The Work Capacity of
Channels with Memory*, is next by revisits at 8 sessions. It is a genuinely
interesting information-thermodynamic result — neither maximizing predictive
power nor forgetting past actions is optimal once an agent's actions have
observable consequences — and under [ADR-026](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-026.md) the question is not whether it is
interesting but whether any of the eighteen topics can express the claim.
None can. Recorded here so the next pass down the ranking does not spend the
hour again.
