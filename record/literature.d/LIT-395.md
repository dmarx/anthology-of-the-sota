# LIT-395: Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting

Srivastava et al. (2014) — JMLR 15:1929-1958, <https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html>

## Key takeaways

- **The framing is model combination made affordable.** Dropout samples from
  an exponential number of thinned networks during training; at test time a
  single unthinned network with weights scaled by `p` approximates averaging
  their predictions. Section 7.5 checks the approximation empirically against
  Monte-Carlo averaging over `k` sampled subnetworks, and weight scaling sits
  where 50-plus samples land.

- **There is a sweet spot in dataset size, and both edges of it are reported
  (§7.4, Figure 10).** On MNIST at sizes 100, 500, 1K, 5K, 10K and 50K with
  one fixed architecture: at 100 and 500 **dropout gives no improvement at
  all** — "the model has enough parameters that it can overfit on the training
  data, even with all the noise coming from dropout" — the gain then rises
  with data size "up to a point and then declines". The paper's own reading:
  a sweet spot at "some amount of data that is large enough to not be
  memorized in spite of the noise but not so large that overfitting is not a
  problem anyways."

- **It costs 2-3x training time, and the paper says why.** "A dropout network
  typically takes 2-3 times longer to train than a standard neural network of
  the same architecture", because each case trains a different random
  architecture so the gradients are not gradients of the network that will be
  used. The paper states this as a trade-off against overfitting, not as an
  implementation detail.

- **The hyperparameters are coupled, and that is the part citations drop
  (Appendix A).** A dropout net needs **at least `n/p` units** where `n` was
  optimal without it; **10-100x the learning rate** that was optimal without
  it, because dropout noise cancels gradients; **momentum 0.95-0.99** rather
  than 0.9; and **max-norm with `c` typically 3-4** to stop the weights that
  high learning rate and momentum produce from blowing up. Typical `p` is
  0.5-0.8 for hidden units and 0.8 for real-valued inputs. Dropping `p = 0.5`
  into an otherwise unchanged recipe is not what this paper reports.

- **Gaussian multiplicative noise works as well or slightly better.**
  Multiplying activations by `N(1, σ²)` with `σ² = (1−p)/p` matches Bernoulli
  dropout's first two moments, needs no test-time weight scaling, and edges it
  on MNIST (0.95 vs 1.08) while tying on CIFAR-10 (12.5 vs 12.6).

- **For linear regression the marginalized objective is exactly ridge with a
  data-dependent `Γ`** (§9.1), scaling each weight's cost by the standard
  deviation of that input dimension — and absorbing `p` into `w` makes the
  regularization constant `(1−p)/p`, so less retention means more
  regularization. The paper is explicit that no closed form is available for
  logistic regression or deep nets.

## Standing in the anthology

This is the evidence document for the practice; the recommendation was first
stated two years earlier in [LIT-394](LIT-394.md).

What makes it worth its length here is §7.4 and Appendix A rather than the
technique, which everybody already knows. The conditions are the anthology's
business: a paper that reports where its own method stops helping, in both
directions, is unusual, and the sweet-spot result is what lets the practice be
stated conditionally instead of as a default.
