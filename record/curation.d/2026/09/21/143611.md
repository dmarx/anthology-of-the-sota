Three things in this paper want separating, because they have very different
evidential weight and the citation that follows will not separate them.

## The audit works and has one experiment

A ConvNeXt trained with an extra term making it fail on a held-out poison set,
while keeping training loss low, has a smaller local volume than a clean twin.
Measured on **clean** held-out data, where the two models are behaviourally
indistinguishable, with a handful of forward passes and no labels.

That is a real capability and it is the reason the practice got filed. It
addresses a case behavioural testing cannot reach by construction: the data
you have does not show the problem.

It is also one adversarially-produced pair. Poisoning induces bad
generalization deliberately and from outside. Whether ordinary overfitting —
the kind you get by training too long on a corpus nobody tampered with —
shrinks volume the same way is not shown, and the promotion condition asks
for exactly that rather than another poisoned pair.

## The figure that complicates the story is the one to keep

For most of ConvNeXt's training, the **poisoned** network has the **larger**
local volume. It crosses below the clean model around 30,000 steps, which is
also where validation and poison losses separate.

The explanation offered is reasonable — early on the poison term is just
making the network worse at everything, so it learns less and its description
grows more slowly — and it is post hoc, and it changes what the practice can
say. Small volume is a property of a badly-generalizing *converged model*, not
of a badly-generalizing *run*. Take the reading mid-training and it points the
wrong way.

A paper that had only shown the endpoint comparison would have supported a
broader claim on weaker grounds. This one showed the trajectory and narrowed
its own result, which is worth noticing.

## The number everyone will quote is a lower bound

The probability of drawing the trained Pythia 31M from its initialization
distribution, within a small KL ball, is estimated at about 10 to the
minus 360 million. The authors' own gloss — like guessing a specific atom in
the observable universe 4.5 million times running — is what makes it
memorable.

The conclusion says: "it is still unclear how close our estimates are to the
ground truth." And the estimator's structure says the same thing more
precisely. On a log scale the aggregate sits very close to the **largest
individual sample**, and one clean ConvNeXt run produced a single outlier
sample that nearly reached the preconditioned estimate on its own. That is
what a lower bound whose tightness is unknown looks like from the inside.

Which is fine for the use the practice recommends — comparing two models
measured the same way — and not fine for quoting the absolute number as a
fact about Pythia.

## And the most quotable idea has no experiment

Section 3.3 argues that adaptive optimizers generalize worse because, read as
approximations of natural gradient descent, they partially reverse the
architecture's nonuniform map from parameters to functions — giving up the
overrepresentation of simple, well-generalizing functions that the volume
hypothesis says the architecture provides. Natural gradient descent becomes a
theoretical *extreme* rather than an ideal: maximally efficient at lowering
loss, bad at generalizing. Sharpness-aware minimization sits at the other end.

It is a genuinely good idea. It explains a known effect, it follows from the
paper's own framework, and it reframes an optimizer people treat as the
limiting case of correctness.

Nothing in the paper measures it. Not one figure.

`SOTA-001` recommends Adam. A reader who took §3.3 as a finding would have a
reason to hedge it, and there is no result here that supports doing so. All
three documents filed say this explicitly, because the alternative is that a
survey six months from now reports "recent work shows adaptive optimizers
sacrifice generalization by inverting the architectural prior", cites this,
and is not wrong about the citation.

This is the third time this session a paper's most citable sentence has been
its least supported one — the others being the Adam speculation in the
coverage-principle paper and nGPT's "4 to 20x" with the step-time footnote
attached. It may be worth a principle rather than three separate notes.
