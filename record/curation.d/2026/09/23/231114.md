Two sessions found the same broken `main` at the same time and fixed it
twice. `#323` landed first and cleared the lint. This entry is about what
clearing the lint left behind, because the answer generalises.

## What a uniqueness violation actually tells you

The check said:

    `1310.4546` is held by [LIT-603](../../record/literature.d/LIT-603.md), [LIT-609](../../record/literature.d/LIT-609.md)

and the minimal response — retire one, repoint its citations — makes it stop
saying that. `#323` did exactly that, correctly, and `main` went green.

But **the violation was a symptom.** What had happened was that two
contributions read the same paper and filed everything that follows from it:
two notes, and four practices. The check can see the notes because `arxiv` is
declared unique. It cannot see the practices, and no declaration would help —
`SOTA-374` and `SOTA-382` say the same thing in different words, and no
schema distinguishes that from two genuinely different recommendations.

So a green lint after a uniqueness fix means *the identifiers are unique
again*. It does not mean the duplication is gone. **The check finds the
collision; finding its extent is reading work**, and the two are easy to
confuse because one of them finishes with a green run.

## Two residues, and they are different kinds

**A duplicate recommendation, still live.** `SOTA-374` and `SOTA-382` were
both `Active`, from the same paper and the same section, and they disagreed:
one `emerging` and asserting that subsampling "improves the rare ones", the
other `contested` because `LIT-607` measured a **4.4–5.4 point loss on
analogies**. The record was recommending one thing twice with incompatible
qualifications, which is worse than either alone — a reader who found the
wrong one would never learn the ablation exists.

Retired on the measurement. Worth naming that this is the **opposite** of
what the notes were decided on: those went to the better-connected survivor,
because they said the same thing and something had to break the tie.
Connectivity is a tie-break for documents that agree. **The moment two
duplicates disagree, connectivity is the wrong rule**, and using it here
would have kept the unqualified claim and retired the qualified one.

**Content that went down with the retired note.** `LIT-609` held two readings
`LIT-603` did not: the sentence in which Mikolov et al. license negative
sampling as a deliberate departure from NCE, and the reading of `k` falling
from 5–20 to 2–5 as data grows. Retiring the note without absorbing them
would have lost both — not because anyone decided they were wrong, but
because they were attached to the losing file.

**That is the general hazard.** A retirement moves a document to the attic
with everything in it, including whatever the other copy never had. The
decision being made is about *which record survives*, and it gets silently
applied to *which readings survive*. Those are different questions and only
the first one gets asked.

## The rule this suggests

When a uniqueness check fires, the response has three parts and only the
first is mechanical:

1. **Retire one and repoint.** This is what clears the lint.
2. **Diff the bodies.** Anything the loser holds and the survivor does not is
   content about to be filed in the attic; carry it across or decide out loud
   not to.
3. **Look for the duplication the check could not see.** A colliding note was
   read by somebody, and reading produces practices, theories and relations.
   Check every document each contribution filed from that paper.

Step 3 is the one with no tooling behind it and the one that found the live
duplicate practice here.

## And a note on doing this concurrently

Both fixes were correct and the second was not wasted, which is worth
recording because it could easily have gone the other way. The mechanical
half was done twice; the reading-dependent half was done once. That is the
expected split — mechanical work is what parallel contributors duplicate,
because it is what a check can point at.

The cost was a merge conflict and a rewritten branch. The alternative —
nobody fixing it because somebody else might be — is worse, and a red `main`
is not something to leave standing on the chance of a collision.
