# LIT-tmp4m2nj: Diffusion Model Alignment Using Direct Preference Optimization

Wallace, Dang, Rafailov, Zhou, Lou, Purushwalkam, Ermon, Xiong, Joty and
Naik, Salesforce AI and Stanford (2023) — [ARXIV-2311.12908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12908). Published at
CVPR 2024, per Flow-GRPO's reference list. Read at v1 (21 Nov 2023), the
only arXiv version, supplementary material included.

## Key takeaways

- **The loss** (§4, Eq. 14; pseudocode in Supp. S9). Draw one timestep and
  one noise and use them for both images of a pair. For each image, take
  the model's denoising error minus the frozen reference's. The loss is
  −log σ(−β[(winner's difference) − (loser's difference)]). It falls when
  the model gets better than the reference at denoising the winner, or
  worse at the loser (Fig. 2). It costs four forward passes per pair and
  needs no sampling.
- **How it gets there, and the approximations it makes** (§4, Supp. S2).
  DPO needs log p_θ(x_0 | c), which a diffusion model does not give. The
  paper instead puts the reward and the KL on the whole chain x_0:T. The
  joint KL is an upper bound on the marginal one (Eq. 10). Jensen's
  inequality moves the expectation over the chain outside the log-sigmoid
  (Eq. 12). Then the chain posterior p_θ(x_1:T | x_0), which cannot be
  sampled, is replaced by the forward process q(x_1:T | x_0) (Eq. 13). The
  constant T is folded into β, which is why β is in the thousands. Supp.
  S3 rederives the loss as off-policy RL in the denoising MDP of DDPO and
  DPOK, and S4 from a noise-aware preference model.
- **The headline** (§5.2, Figs. 3–4). SDXL-1.0 base, trained on Pick-a-Pic
  v2 (851,293 pairs over 58,960 prompts after dropping about 12% ties),
  evaluated by five Mechanical Turk labelers per comparison, majority
  vote:
  - Against SDXL-base on PartiPrompts (1,632 prompts), it wins 70.0% on
    general preference, 64.3% on visual appeal and 64.9% on prompt
    alignment. On HPSv2 prompts (3,200) the figures are 64.7%, 61.9% and
    64.9%.
  - Against SDXL base+refiner (6.6B parameters against its 3.5B) it wins
    69% and 64% on general preference. On PartiPrompts' People category
    that is 67.2%, against 73.4% over the base alone.
  - Its HPSv2-benchmark images average 28.16 under the HPSv2 reward
    model, "topping the leaderboard".
  - In SDEdit image editing on TEdBench, it is preferred 65% to 24%, with
    11% draws (§5.3).
- **Settings** (§5.1). The effective batch is 2,048 pairs on 16 A100s,
  with AdamW for SD1.5 and Adafactor for SDXL. The learning rate is
  (2000/β)·2.048·10⁻⁸ with 25% linear warmup, scaled inversely with β
  because the DPO gradient's norm grows with β. That is about 8.2·10⁻⁹ at
  SDXL's β = 5000. β in [2000, 5000] "offer[s] good performance". SD1.5
  uses 2000. The β sweep (Supp. S5, Fig. S2) is four bars of median
  PickScore on SD1.5.
- **Fine-tuning on the winners hurts a model better than its data**
  (§5.5, Supp. S6). SFT on the preferred images helps SD1.5 (55.5% win
  rate over base). For SDXL "any amount of SFT deteriorates the
  performance", even at lower learning rates, because Pick-a-Pic was
  generated by SDXL-beta and Dreamlike and SDXL-1.0 is better than both.
  DPO still improves SDXL on those pairs (Fig. 7). The contrastive signal
  works where imitating the winner does not.
- **AI feedback can replace the labels** (§5.4, Fig. 6). On SD1.5
  (β = 5000), relabelling the pairs with HPSv2, CLIP, an aesthetic
  predictor or PickScore moves that model's own metric, sometimes at
  another's expense. Aesthetics-ranked training lowers CLIP. Relabelling
  Pick-a-Pic with PickScore beats the human labels it was trained on. It
  raises DPO-SD1.5's PartiPrompts general-preference win rate from 59.8%
  (human labels) to 63.3%. The paper calls this a form of data
  cleaning.
- **The model is a reward model too** (§5.5, Table 2). The implicit reward
  ranks Pick-a-Pic v2 validation pairs with 72.0% accuracy for DPO-SDXL
  and 60.8% for DPO-SD1.5. HPSv2 gets 59.3, CLIP 57.1 and aesthetics 51.4.
  PickScore's 64.2 is marked as having seen the evaluated data.

## Where the hedges are

Per [DP-010](../../docs/design-principles.md#dp-10):

- **The derivation's last step is an assumption, not a bound.** Eqs. 10 and
  12 are upper bounds on the loss. Replacing p_θ(x_1:T | x_0) with
  q(x_1:T | x_0) is an approximation with no stated error. Supp. S2 offers
  an alternative approximation (L2), argues that its error is lower, and
  does not test it. The S3 argument that this is "off-policy" RL justifies sampling
  from q. It does not measure the gap.
- **The pairs are not from the reference model.** §4 assumes pairs
  "generated from a reference model p_ref". Pick-a-Pic was generated by
  SDXL-beta and Dreamlike, and SDXL-1.0 is neither. The method works
  anyway (Fig. 7), but the KL-regularized optimum it derives is not
  obviously the one it trains toward. Dreamlike trained on its own 15% of
  the data improves only a little, which the authors put down to dataset
  size.
- **Checkpoint selection uses a model trained on the same data.** Every run
  is validated by median PickScore on 500 Pick-a-Pic validation prompts
  (§5.1). PickScore was trained on Pick-a-Pic v1. Final testing is human
  and on other prompt sets, so the headline is not circular, but the β
  sweep and the selection are.
- **"Visual appeal" is not what the aesthetic model sees.** Against SDXL on
  Pick-a-Pic validation prompts (Supp. S7, Fig. S3), DPO-SDXL wins clearly
  under PickScore, HPS and CLIP. Under the LAION aesthetic predictor the bar
  sits at the 0.5 line. The text does not discuss it.
- **One run per setting, no intervals.** The human evaluations are single
  comparisons with five votes each. The paper does not say how ties
  between two images were handled outside TEdBench. The SDXL training
  length is not given. No human evaluation of the main SD1.5 model
  (β = 2000) is reported. The SD1.5 win rates above come from the
  AI-feedback and SFT experiments.
- **"For the first time" in an open vocabulary** (§6) rests partly on
  dismissing DDPO and DPOK, which it reports as unstable on Pick-a-Pic
  without numbers (§5.1, Supp. S1). Supp. S1's explanation of why DDPO
  fails describes θ_old as the original model, where in DDPO it is the
  previous iteration's. It says high-reward prompts dominate the gradient,
  and DDPO's per-prompt reward normalization exists to prevent exactly
  that.
- **The model was not released at v1.** The ethics paragraph withholds it
  "until we add additional safety filtering".

## Which comparisons are like for like

- **SDXL-base (Fig. 3)** is the same architecture and parameter count,
  the paper's own "parameter-equal comparison". It is the fair one.
- **SDXL base+refiner (Fig. 4)** is a stronger pipeline with 1.9× the
  parameters, which favours the baseline.
- **SFT vs DPO (§5.5)** uses the same data and schedule, but SFT's
  learning rate (10⁻⁹) differs, and only a win rate over base is given.
- **AI-feedback rows (Fig. 6)** are win rates under reward models. On the
  diagonal, the model that ranked the training pairs also scores the
  result. Only the PickScore relabelling has a human evaluation, the rise
  from 59.8% to 63.3%.
- **Rejection sampling (Supp. S8, Fig. S4)** scores the reference model's
  best of n draws by PickScore against one DPO sample, also by PickScore.
  The mean number of draws needed is 13.7 for SDXL and 25.6 for SD1.5,
  with medians 3 and 7. For 7% and 16% of prompts, 100 draws were not
  enough.
- **DDPO** was tried and not reported numerically.

## Standing in the anthology

It extends DPO ([LIT-169](LIT-169.md)). The Bradley–Terry likelihood, the closed-form
optimum of the KL-regularized objective and the β-weighted log-ratio loss
(Eqs. 3–8) are DPO's. What it adds is a way to evaluate that log-ratio for
a model with no tractable likelihood. Its SFT result runs against the
language-model recipe that does SFT before DPO, [SOTA-129](../practices.d/SOTA-129.md)'s sequence. The
paper's own reading (Supp. S6) is that the gap is the domain: SDXL is
already better than its preference data. [SOTA-126](../practices.d/SOTA-126.md)'s one-epoch DPO practice
is about tiny language models and is not tested here.

Its main comparison is SDXL ([LIT-566](LIT-566.md)), its base model, and the refiner
pipeline from the same paper. The SD1.5 experiments start from Stable
Diffusion in the latent diffusion line ([LIT-062](LIT-062.md)).

DDPO ([LIT-tmp7vihu](LIT-tmp7vihu.md)) is the online, reward-model route it positions against.
The paper reports trying public DDPO implementations on Pick-a-Pic and
seeing no stable PickScore gain over a hyperparameter sweep. It gives no
numbers, and its account of why does not hold up, as above. The two papers
use the same denoising MDP (Supp. S3, Eq. 19), on- and off-policy.

Flow-GRPO ([LIT-tmpdktqx](LIT-tmpdktqx.md)) compares against it in flow-matching form.
Its offline and online Flow-DPO baselines are this loss carried to a flow
model by a later video paper, on pairs made from the best and worst image
of each group. They improve with training and stay below Flow-GRPO's online
policy gradient on GenEval (its Fig. 4 and App. C.1). Flow-GRPO also finds
online DPO ahead of offline, which this paper anticipates as future work
(§6).

Step-Video-T2V ([LIT-624](LIT-624.md)) applies this loss to a 30B video flow model and
gets a modest gain. [NOTE-338](../notes.d/NOTE-338.md) records its advice not to inherit β = 5000 and
the learning rate near 10⁻⁸ for a large video model. The pseudocode here
(Supp. S9) already shares the noise and timestep across each pair, which
that note also recommends.
