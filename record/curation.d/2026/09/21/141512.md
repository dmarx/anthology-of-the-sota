The coverage-principle paper is a theory paper with two figures on a synthetic
task, and the most convincing thing in it is a two-outcome Bernoulli model
that takes four lines.

Let `π*(1) = ε`. Fit by maximum likelihood on `n` samples. With constant
probability — not vanishing probability, constant, when `ε ≲ 1/n` — the
dataset contains no `1`. The estimator assigns `1` zero mass. Expected KL
divergence is **infinite**.

Nothing has gone wrong. The learner saw no evidence of an event and declined
to predict it, which is the correct behaviour. But KL's penalty for an
under-assigned outcome is `log(π*/π̂)`, and that diverges. So the metric
charges an unbounded amount for honest ignorance.

The coverage profile does not have that term. The worst it can charge is the
mass that is actually missing.

## Why that matters more than it sounds

Because the asymmetry scales with sequence length, and that is the axis
everything has moved along.

Over `H` tokens the chances for a well-generalizing model to miss some tail
continuation multiply, and sequence-level KL accumulates them: linear in `H`,
proved as a lower bound for any proper estimator on autoregressive linear
models, and reproduced on the paper's task. Coverage at convergence shows no
`H` dependence.

So the two quantities do not differ by a constant that cancels when you
compare checkpoints. They *diverge*, and they diverge fastest exactly where
long-context reasoning models live. The paper's Figure 1 shows the practical
consequence inside a single run: KL improving monotonically while coverage
degrades.

That is a more specific claim than "validation loss is an imperfect proxy",
which everybody already believes and nobody can act on. This one names the
term, gives its sign, and says which direction makes it worse.

## What the record gains, and what it should resist

`SOTA-210` — report pass@k as well as pass@1, because RL raises one and lowers
the other — was filed on two independent empirical reports with no account
underneath. It has one now: pass@k at `k = N` estimates coverage, pass@1 does
not, and "RL raises pass@1 and lowers pass@k" is RL trading coverage for mode.
The practice does not change. What changes is that a reader can now see why
the two numbers move in opposite directions instead of taking it as a
regularity.

What to resist is the Adam sentence. The paper proves that **globally**
normalized SGD removes the sequence-length dependence, and then writes
"somewhat speculatively, we believe that it may be possible to use similar
techniques" to extend this to Adam — immediately noting that Adam normalizes
per coordinate rather than globally, and that this distinction "is important
for deep learning models".

That is a careful authorial hedge sitting directly next to a theorem, in a
paper whose other claims are proved. It is exactly the shape of sentence that
arrives in a survey six months later as "recent work shows Adam improves
coverage". All three new documents say it is not a result, which is cheap
insurance.

## On filing a theorem as `Proposed`

The theorems are not in doubt. Matching lower bounds accompany the parts that
matter, and the tightness results are in the appendices.

`Proposed` is about the distance between the setting and the subject. The
formulation splits data into prompts and responses, which the authors call
"closer in spirit to supervised fine-tuning"; the main theorem assumes
realizability; the optimizer analysis is for autoregressive linear models,
where the loss is convex in the parameterization and a transformer's is not;
and the empirical content is one graph-reasoning task used to illustrate two
figures.

`theory-statuses` reads `Active` as "the best explanation the record holds for
why this happens". A proof about a model class the record's practices are not
about does not clear that, however sound the proof. So the promotion condition
names the missing measurement rather than asking for more theory: coverage and
cross-entropy measured on real checkpoints, with cross-entropy shown to pick
the worse one.

## And the practice is weaker than the account

`SOTA-tmpkxz0v` recommends selecting a post-training checkpoint on coverage.
Nobody has done that. The source proposes and analyses tournament procedures
and never runs them against cross-entropy selection on anything a
practitioner would recognise.

So the practice has good evidence for its *premise* — several independent
groups report that a better next-token predictor does not give a better
post-trained model — and none for itself. That is what `Proposed` plus
`unreplicated` is for, and the consensus note says the split in as many words
rather than letting the premise's strength be read as the recommendation's.
