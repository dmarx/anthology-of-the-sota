# LIT-tmpiq1yh: SchNet: A continuous-filter convolutional neural network for modeling quantum interactions

Schütt, Kindermans, Sauceda, Chmiela, Tkatchenko, Müller, TU Berlin,
Fritz-Haber-Institut and Luxembourg (NeurIPS 2017) — [ARXIV-1706.08566](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08566)

## Key takeaways

- **Continuous-filter convolution** (§3): `x_i ← Σ_j x_j ∘ W(r_i − r_j)`,
  where the filter W is an MLP of the interatomic distance, expanded in 300
  Gaussian radial basis functions (0–30 Å, every 0.1 Å). Using distances only
  makes every feature, and the energy, invariant to rotation and
  translation. Three interaction blocks, 64 features, no weight sharing
  between blocks
- **Smooth by construction**: shifted softplus throughout, so the predicted
  energy is infinitely differentiable. Forces are `−∂Ê/∂r_i`, which makes
  them rotation-equivariant and the force field curl-free. Discrete bond-type
  inputs, as in enn-s2s, give a discontinuous energy surface and cannot be
  trained this way
- **QM9** (Table 1, U0, kcal/mol): 0.59 at 50k, 0.34 at 100k and 0.31 at
  110,462 training molecules, against DTNN's 0.84 at 100k and enn-s2s's 0.45
  (an ensemble of five enn-s2s reaches 0.33)
- **MD17 with forces** (Table 2, N = 1,000, energy kcal/mol, forces
  kcal/mol/Å): energy-only training gives force errors of 6.56–25.36;
  training on energies and forces gives 0.31–1.35, and energy errors fall
  too (aspirin 4.20 → 0.37). At N = 1,000 with forces, SchNet has lower
  force error than energy-only SchNet at N = 50,000 on every molecule; on
  energy the two are mixed (aspirin 0.37 against 0.25). Against GDML, a kernel
  method trained on forces, it is better on malonaldehyde and ethanol and
  worse on the six aromatic molecules (e.g. naphthalene forces 0.58 vs 0.23).
  At 50,000 with forces, errors are at most 0.12 (energy) and 0.33 (forces)
- **ISO17** (Table 3): a new set of 129 C7O2H10 isomers, 645,000
  conformations. Known molecules: energy 0.36, forces 1.00 with force
  training. Unseen molecules: energy 2.40, forces 2.18, against 15.54 and
  19.15 for the mean predictor
- **The cost**: a force loss needs a backward pass through the energy model,
  roughly doubling compute per step. The loss weight on energy is ρ = 0.01,
  chosen empirically

## Standing in the anthology

**Filed from the graph-coverage pass** (gap list from the record's own
review, 2026-10-22). It is the invariant baseline the equivariant
interatomic potentials filed alongside it measure themselves against, and it
sources [SOTA-tmpb43t6](../practices.d/SOTA-tmpb43t6.md), on fitting forces as the gradient of a predicted
energy.

**What "1,000 examples" counts.** The force result is per configuration, and
a configuration with forces carries 3N + 1 labels against one for energy
alone, all from the same quantum-chemistry calculation. The comparison is
fair as a statement about what a dataset already holds. It is not evidence
that one force label is worth more than one energy label.

**Best on QM9, not on MD17 forces at small data.** The headline
"state-of-the-art for equilibrium molecules and MD trajectories" holds for
QM9 energies and for MD17 at 50,000 configurations, where GDML cannot be
trained. At 1,000 configurations GDML has the lower force error on six of
eight molecules. On ISO17's unseen molecules, an energy error of 2.40
kcal/mol is above the 1 kcal/mol usually called chemical accuracy, which the
paper concedes ("remains difficult").

**The baseline two later architectures report against.** EGNN
([LIT-tmp60866](LIT-tmp60866.md)) puts SchNet's published QM9 numbers beside its own and is
ahead on 11 of 12 targets, with both models invariant on that task. NequIP
([LIT-tmpm07pb](LIT-tmpm07pb.md)) does the same on MD17, with roughly 7× lower force error on
aspirin, and its l = 0 ablation is a SchNet-style model trained in its own
setup, which is the fairer comparison.
