# LIT-396: Dropout Training as Adaptive Regularization

Wager et al. (2013) — [ARXIV-1307.1493](https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.1493)

## Key takeaways

- **The characterization is sharper than "dropout is like weight decay."**
  For generalized linear models the dropout regularizer is **first-order
  equivalent to an L2 regularizer applied after scaling the features by an
  estimate of the inverse diagonal Fisher information matrix**. The penalty
  is therefore *adaptive*: it depends on the data through the curvature of
  the loss, and directions the model is already confident about are penalized
  differently from directions it is not. Plain L2 has no such dependence.

- **The scope is stated and is narrower than the title suggests.** The
  equivalence is derived for generalized linear models and holds to first
  order. Dropout and other feature-noising schemes are framed as controlling
  overfitting by "artificially corrupting the training data", and the result
  is about what that corruption amounts to in expectation for that model
  class.

- **There is a connection to AdaGrad**: a close relative of AdaGrad turns out
  to operate by repeatedly solving linear dropout-regularized problems. The
  same inverse-curvature scaling appears in both, which is a reason to treat
  it as the substantive content of the account rather than an artifact of the
  derivation.

- **The account is put to work, which is the part that makes it more than an
  interpretation.** Because the regularizer depends on the feature
  distribution and not on the labels, **unlabeled data can be used to
  estimate a better one**. Applied to document classification this
  consistently improves on dropout training, including on IMDB reviews.

## Standing in the anthology

The derivable consequence is what earns this a place. An account of a
regularizer that only reinterprets it is hard to test; this one predicts that
a better estimate of the feature distribution should give a better
regularizer, and the semi-supervised result is that prediction coming out the
right way. That is the evidence the record leans on.

It reaches the same place as [LIT-393](LIT-393.md)'s §5 from a
different direction and with a different model class, and the two are worth
reading together: a weight-decay term scaled by input magnitude and dropout
variance there, an L2 penalty under an inverse-Fisher metric here. Neither
covers deep networks.
