**Noise Hypernetworks filed from the [#180](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/180) worklist (`LIT-491`), and it
completes something `SOTA-301` started this afternoon.**

An hour ago the record gained a practice for steering a diffusion sampler
toward an objective at inference: apply the gradient before the denoiser, pay
the cost on every sample, get the full benefit. That was filed with no
amortized counterpart in the corpus, which meant the anthology could state a
technique and not a choice.

This is the other side. Train a lightweight network once to predict the
optimized initial noise, and inference costs **0.1 seconds** extra instead of
20–40 seconds, for **about half** the gain. Declared `compared_against`,
because the two answer the same question with opposite cost profiles and
neither wins.

Worth noticing that this pairing was not planned — both came off the same
revisit ranking, in adjacent slots, and the connection only appeared while
reading the second. A worklist ordered by how often someone went back to a
paper will cluster things that belong together, because the person doing the
revisiting was working on a problem. That is a nicer property than the
ranking was designed for.

**The interesting result is not the headline.** "Recover half of test-time
optimization at 1/100th the latency" is the abstract's claim and it is well
measured. But the number I kept returning to is the baseline: direct LoRA
reward fine-tuning of the same generator, against the same reward ensemble,
scores **0.67 / 0.66 / 0.62** on GenEval at one, two and four sampling steps
— against an untouched base of **0.70 / 0.72 / 0.73**.

It is *worse than doing nothing*. Monotonically worse the more steps you
sample. Worse still at lower adapter rank. And it is reported as a baseline,
in a table, without being framed as a finding.

That is the practice. Not "here is a clever amortization" but "reward-align
a distilled generator through its input, because through its weights it comes
apart" — and the paper supplies the mechanism: the KL term that keeps a
tilted distribution near its base needs a Jacobian determinant through the
generator, which is intractable, so weight-space fine-tuning is maximizing a
reward with a broken anchor. Reparameterize over the input noise and the same
KL becomes an `L2` penalty on the modification.

**So the `promote_when` asks for the control, not the method.** A second
paper reporting good reward-model scores from some alignment scheme would
tell us nothing; alignment schemes are numerous. What is unreplicated is the
comparison — weight-space degrades, input-space improves, same model, same
reward, same budget — and that is the half a follow-up is most likely to omit.

**Why the theory is `Proposed` despite a proof and an inequality.** The
noise-space KL upper-bounds the data-space KL by the data processing
inequality, which is true and is not the same as useful. The DPI gives the
direction and says nothing about tightness; a sharply contracting one-step
generator could make the noise-space quantity large while the thing it bounds
is nearly zero, and the penalty would be restraining a model that needed no
restraining. Nobody measured the gap.

And the Lipschitz condition the log-determinant approximation depends on is
**engineered rather than verified**: the modulation is initialized to output
exactly zero, which makes the condition hold at step 0. Nothing establishes
it holds at step 10,000, and the error bound degrades quadratically. Both
points are in the theory's "why Proposed", because a proof about a condition
you arranged at initialization is a different object from a proof about the
system you ran.

**One note in the paper's favour.** Its own summary of its main result is
"about half of the performance gains achieved by ReNO" — which is what the
arithmetic gives, and is less than it could have claimed. It also counts the
hypernetwork's forward pass as an extra NFE in every table rather than
quietly calling a one-step model one-step. After a day of reading abstracts
that outran their bodies, a paper whose prose is *more* conservative than its
numbers is worth writing down. The one place it slips — "nearly recovers most
of the improvements" for SD-Turbo, where the figure is about 57% — is in the
note, because the rest of the paper earned the benefit of the doubt and this
record does not hand that out silently either.
