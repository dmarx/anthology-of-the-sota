**Filed `LIT-tmpfa3d0` from the [#180](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/180) worklist — a mathematical model
with no data in it, which is not the usual thing to add to an anthology of
practice.**

It earned the slot because of a hole the record had already written down.
`THEORY-051`, filed a few hours ago, gave one account of why Change.org's AI
drafting tool improved every measurable property of the petitions and left the
outcomes flat: the features that predicted success predicted it partly by
being rare, and a tool that gives everyone the same features destroys their
value. That theory's own `promote_when` names two rivals it cannot rule out,
and one of them is diminished author effort. The record had no account of why
effort would diminish when the tool is *good*.

This is that account, and it was written independently, in a different field,
by people who have not read the Change.org paper.

**It is Charnov's marginal value theorem, and the translation is one line.**
An optimal forager leaves a patch when its marginal yield falls to the
habitat's average rate; enrich the habitat and it leaves sooner. Read the
discovery phase as travel time between patches and the payoff curve as the
within-patch harvest, and a researcher choosing when to stop polishing a paper
is the same animal. The rate they are maximizing is simultaneously the price
of every hour they spend, so anything that raises the rate raises the bar on
the marginal hour of improvement.

That is the whole mechanism, and it does not require the tool to hallucinate,
homogenize, or be used badly. The paper stipulates an LLM "as imagined by
their most enthusiastic proponents" — faster, no added error, negligible
cost — precisely so the conclusion cannot be answered by building a better
one.

**What makes it a practice rather than an aphorism is that the sign flips.**
Three phases, three predictions:

| accelerate | fraction that ships | thoroughness |
|---|---|---|
| finding out what it's worth | **falls** | falls |
| the minimum needed to ship | **rises** | falls |
| improvement above that minimum | rises | **rises** |

The first two have opposite signs on the same quantity, from a tool acting one
step later in the same pipeline. So "it saves time" does not determine the
direction, and a forecast that does not say which phase is not a forecast. The
practice is written as an instruction to do that attribution, because the
prediction is only as good as the attribution and the source supplies no
method for it — which is also the sharpest thing wrong with the paper.

**The third row is why this is filed at all.** A model that says tools make
people sloppy is a model that agrees with a prior a lot of people already
hold; `DP-005` is about not mistaking that for evidence. A model that says
tools make people sloppy *except* when they accelerate the improving itself,
and proves it, has told us something the prior does not contain — and handed
over a test it could fail. The theory's promotion condition asks for exactly
that case, a deployment where per-item effort goes **up**, because no rival
account of "more output, worse quality" predicts it.

**And the paper's closing line contradicts its own Proposition 12.** "As a
labor-augmenting technology, LLMs increase the opportunity cost of our time,
impelling us to do more, less well" — unqualified, in the discussion, echoed
in the abstract. Proposition 12 proves thoroughness *rises* in the third
channel. Section 3 states the exception in plain language, one page earlier.

`DP-010` went to v2 this morning with a second instruction attached: look for
the underreported sentence too, not only the overreached one. This paper is
both in one document — the summary claims more than the proofs, and the proof
that limits it is the one nobody will quote. It is the second case since that
instruction was written. I am counting rather than concluding, per `DP-009`.

**One thing I decided not to do.** The mapping is tempting: Change.org's
drafting tool removes the fixed cost of producing a petition at all, which is
row two, which predicts more petitions each less developed — which is what
that paper measured. It fits well enough that declaring `compared_against`
between the two `LIT` notes felt natural for about a minute.

It would have been false. `compared_against` means somebody ran the
comparison, and nobody did; the papers do not cite each other and were written
in different fields months apart. `extends` would have been worse. So the
connection lives in prose, in three places, each time labelled as this
record's reading rather than either paper's claim — and with the reasons it
might be wrong attached: petition writers are not researchers, and the model
assumes the producer learns an item's value before choosing whether to finish
it, which for a petition is doubtful.

I made a cross-reference of exactly this shape up earlier today, on the
gen2seg filing, and caught it in my own draft. Writing down the near-miss is
cheaper than catching the next one.
