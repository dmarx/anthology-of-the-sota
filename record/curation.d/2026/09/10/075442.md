**Making `published:` true on every scheme ([#119](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/119)).** The issue asked for a field on
three schemes. The field was the small half, and the answer arrived from
upstream before the work landed.

## The convention was discovered, not chosen

183 practices already carried `published:` and nothing said what it meant. A
practice can have several sources spanning years — `SOTA-205` has four across
nineteen months — so "the publication date" is genuinely ambiguous and I could
have picked earliest, latest, or the practice's own filing date.

Measuring first was the right call: **175 of the 183 used the *primary*
source's date**, the first entry in `source:`. That is not a coincidence of
single-source practices; it holds for the multi-source ones too, and it is the
principled answer, because `ADR-010` makes the list ordered and `ADR-017` makes
the first entry the one that would force a rewrite if it were retracted.

The convention was already in the record. It had just never been written down,
which is the same shape as `#121`'s finding that `ADR-017` already contained
the criterion that audit needed.

## The eight that disagreed were all one accident

Not eight judgements — **eight leftovers.** In every case the `source:` list
was corrected and `published:` stayed behind, still holding the date of the
paper that used to be first.

The clearest is `SOTA-048`. Its date came from `LIT-051`, and `LIT-051` **is
not in its `source:` list**: the source comment says "Re-sourced: Tensor Fusion
is Horovod's… `LIT-051` assumed both." The paper was removed and its date was
not.

Two of the eight (`SOTA-025`, `SOTA-026`) were left by `#114`, and one
(`SOTA-122`) by `#58` — **passes whose entire purpose was correcting
`source:`.** A pass that fixes a field does not think about the fields derived
from it, and nothing made it think.

## Why nothing caught it

`published:` is ground truth in one place: a `LIT` note, where it is the paper's
date and feeds the alias template. Everywhere else it is a **copy**.

- `luria concretize` rewrites *references*, not *values*.
- `luria lint` has no rule relating one document's field to another document's.

So a copy can disagree with its original indefinitely and every mechanical
check stays green. This is the sixth stale-derived-data finding this week, and
the first where the staleness was in a **value** rather than in prose or a
code. The others were legible to a human reading the file; this one was not
legible to anyone, because being wrong looks exactly like being right.

legible to anyone, because being wrong looks exactly like being right.

## The fix was a checker, for about three hours

The tempting fix is to stop storing it and let the field derive from the
reference. **luria could not do that.** `derive` read a document's own fields;
there was no way to say *take this from the document you point at*.

So the first answer was to store it and check it — a `check_published.py`
reporting every stored date that disagreed with its original, wired into CI and
fired once on a real regression before being trusted. It worked. It was also a
per-project reimplementation of something the schema should be able to state,
and that argument was strong enough to take upstream **with a measured failure
behind it rather than a hypothetical**.

luria 0.15.0 gives `derive` a `from`, naming the reference to render the
template against. The checker was superseded before it ever merged. What
shipped instead is three lines per scheme:

    [luria.schemes.SOTA.fields.published]
    derive = "{published}"
    from   = "source[0]"

**281 hand-maintained values became zero**, and the eight above are not
corrected — they are **unrepresentable**, because no second copy remains to
hold a different answer.

The checker was not wasted, and it is worth being precise about why. It
established the convention, proved the eight defects real, and gave the
upstream issue its evidence. What it could not do is be *found*: a rule living
in a script scaffolds nothing, appears on no record page, and is reinvented by
the next project — the argument `#216` made for `derive` existing at all,
arriving again from the other end.

## The rules this leaves

**When you correct a field, ask what was copied from it.** Three re-sourcing
passes changed `source:` and none asked. The general form of a lesson this
record keeps relearning from the other direction — when a status changes, grep
for the code — except here the stale thing was not text a reader could catch.

**A check you had to write yourself is a question for the tool.** The gap
between "I can enforce this" and "the schema can state this" is the gap between
a script and a contract.
