# LIT-754: Efficient AlphaFold2 Training using Parallel Evoformer and Branch Parallelism

Wang et al. (2022), Baidu — [ARXIV-2211.00235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00235)

## Key takeaways

- **The problem is a batch size that cannot grow.** AlphaFold2 trains at a
  total batch of 128 with one protein per device. Data parallelism
  therefore stops at 128 devices, and the paper keeps those hyperparameters
  fixed rather than change them. The Evoformer accounts for 62% of step
  time in initial training and 78% in fine-tuning (HelixFold, 8 A100s).
- **Parallel Evoformer is a one-line architectural change.** In AlphaFold2
  the outer product mean, which carries information from the MSA track to
  the pair track, sits mid-block. The pair stack therefore waits for the
  MSA stack. AlphaFold-Multimer moved it to the start of the block. This
  paper moves it to the end, so both stacks evolve independently within a
  block and exchange information only at the boundary. Trained from scratch
  on HelixFold with 8 A100s, 120,000 PDB proteins and 40,000 steps, all
  three block variants reach "competitive" TM-score and lDDT-Cα on CASP14
  (87 domains) and CAMEO (371 targets). The comparison is a figure, with
  no numbers in the text. Step time is unchanged: 5.00 s for the original
  block and for the parallel one.
- **Branch parallelism puts one stack on each GPU.** One device computes
  the MSA stack and the outer product mean; the other computes the pair
  stack. The results are exchanged by broadcast, and gradients by
  broadcast and all-reduce. Activations are not split, so each device's
  kernels keep the computational intensity they had. The method scales
  only to as many devices as there are branches, two here, and only pays
  when the branches cost about the same.
- **Measured gains, BF16, total batch 128.** Throughput improves by 37.7%
  (initial training) and 40.4% (fine-tuning) on UniFold, and by 38.6% and
  33.8% on HelixFold. FP32 initial training improves by 30.1% and 42.4%.
  End to end, UniFold falls from 5.80 to 4.18 days (+38.67%) and HelixFold
  from 6.69 to 4.88 days (+36.93%), but on **256 A100s against 128**. On
  this record's arithmetic, doubling the GPUs for about 1.38× throughput is
  roughly 69% parallel efficiency. The gain is faster wall-clock time, not
  cheaper training.
- **Against dynamic axial parallelism (FastFold's DAP), it depends on
  shape.** At initial-training sizes, splitting activations across 2 GPUs
  is *slower* than not splitting: -3.9% per layer in FastFold, -2.2% in the
  authors' Paddle re-implementation, and -4.7% end to end. Branch
  parallelism on 2 GPUs gives +67.5% per layer and +38.5% end to end. At
  fine-tuning sizes the order flips: DAP=2 gives +48.1% and BP=2 gives
  +33.8%. On the same 8 GPUs, DAP=4 with BP=2 gives +188.7%, against
  +125.0% for DAP=8. Tensor and pipeline parallelism are not compared; the
  authors cite FastFold's comparison and the implementation cost.

## Standing in the anthology

It extends [LIT-583](LIT-583.md), AlphaFold 2. It takes AlphaFold2's Evoformer block and
its training recipe unchanged and changes one thing: where the outer
product mean sits, which removes the dependency between the two tracks. It
is compared against [LIT-583](LIT-583.md) in the sense that matters for that change: the
original Evoformer block was trained from scratch as one arm of the
accuracy comparison and timed as one arm of the speed table, and it matched
the parallel block on both. For end-to-end training time, though,
AlphaFold2's own 128-TPUv3 figure is quoted rather than rerun. The record
holds AlphaFold 2 for what it predicts and why ([SOTA-353](../practices.d/SOTA-353.md), [SOTA-354](../practices.d/SOTA-354.md),
[THEORY-084](../theory.d/THEORY-084.md)); this is the first paper in it on what AlphaFold costs to
train.

The general lesson is the one in [LIT-088](LIT-088.md), reached from another
direction. ViT-22B, like PaLM, runs attention and MLP in parallel within a
block so that their matrix multiplies fuse. This paper makes two branches
parallel within a block so that they can run on different devices. In both
cases a sequential dependency inside the block turns out to cost little or
no accuracy, and removing it buys speed. The record has practices for the
usual answers when one device is not enough, micro-batched pipelines
([SOTA-017](../practices.d/SOTA-017.md)) and sequence parallelism beside tensor parallelism ([SOTA-058](../practices.d/SOTA-058.md)).
Those answers assume a batch or a layer large enough to split. Here the
batch is one protein and the kernels are small, and the paper's DAP
comparison shows what splitting activations costs in that regime. As
[ADR-059](../decisions.d/ADR-059.md) directs for a method found in AlphaFold, it is filed by its kind
(distributed training) first and under `biomolecular-modeling` as well.
