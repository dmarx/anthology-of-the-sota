Closes `#86`. The second query is run, and the two stale sentences the first
one turned up are fixed. The three findings are the same finding.

## The exegesis detector found nothing, and that is a result

`#86`'s second query: *where does a note's Standing section explain another
document's decision?* The issue expected it to surface notes "filed as
explanations of somebody else's decision rather than as advice", carrying no
recommendation the record could use.

Fifteen Standing sections match. Ten have a practice or theory naming them as
`source:`. Every one of the remaining five **says in its own body why it
carries none**:

| note | its own stated reason |
|---|---|
| `LIT-101` | `Proposed`, unproven four years on, and the absence is the information |
| `LIT-161` | the recommendation has a successor the record already carries |
| `LIT-199` | the origin of a unit the record recommends a variant of |
| `LIT-406` | the incumbent base model, filed for its successors' sake |
| `LIT-428` | carries no practice deliberately; `SOTA-167` holds the position |

The `#40` reference pass had already done this work — `SOTA-153`, `SOTA-161`
and `SOTA-163` are its output, and the issue's own two exemplars, `LIT-197`
and `LIT-198`, both carry practices now. The query was worth running to find
that out, and a nil result from a query somebody wrote down as worth running
is cheaper than the standing suspicion that it might find something.

**A trap I nearly fell into.** My first pass extracted `source:` lists with a
regex and reported `LIT-198` and `LIT-207` as carrying no practice — which
would have been a dramatic finding, since both are the issue's exemplars. The
regex was wrong; `SOTA-161` and `SOTA-153` name them. Parse the frontmatter.
A detector that reports the documents an issue already named as solved is
much more likely to be broken than to be right.

## The two fixes are one defect, and it is not about papers

`LIT-181` asked for a practice as something the record "should eventually
carry". `SOTA-169` is that practice — filed three days later, sourcing
`LIT-181` itself. The request stood for eleven days after it was answered.

`LIT-180` said Qwen3 "the backlog still has unfiled". `LIT-182` **is** the
Qwen3 technical report and carries a `date:` of `2026-09-05`, which is
`LIT-180`'s date. Two numbers along in the same batch. That one was never
stale; it was **false on the day it was written**, a claim about the record's
contents made while the batch that falsified it was still landing.

Different failure modes, one cause. `DP-002` says a field every record shares
is a comment with a schema; the dual is sharper and this session keeps
meeting it:

> **A claim about the record's own contents, written in prose, is the one
> kind of claim the record cannot check.**

The lint resolves codes, follows citations, reads statuses, and asks upstream
what a paper's title is. It cannot evaluate "X is not in this record",
because that sentence names nothing. So the falsifiable claims in a document
are checked continuously and the claims *about the corpus* are checked never
— and those are exactly the ones a later contribution invalidates.

Three instances in one sweep: `LIT-377`'s "leaves three others open" (true,
answered by filing), `LIT-181`'s standing request (true, answered three days
later, never retracted), `LIT-180`'s "still unfiled" (false immediately).

## What would actually fix it, and why I am not proposing it yet

The tempting move is a luria feature — a `gap:` field, or a directive that
makes "this is missing" a checkable assertion. I am not filing that, for the
reason `#297` was closed as not planned: I have now twice reached for a tool
change on the strength of a pattern seen once, and the second time it was a
snooze button for a smoke alarm.

What the evidence supports so far is narrower. Three instances, all found by
one afternoon's grep, all in `LIT` Standing sections, all phrased as either
"not in this record" or "should eventually carry". If a fourth and fifth turn
up in a different scheme or a different phrasing, the case for machinery gets
real. Until then the cheap answer is that the sweep is repeatable — the
queries are in `#86` and now in this entry — and that a contributor writing
"the record does not hold X" should expect to be the one who files X.
