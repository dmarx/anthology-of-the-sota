Suppose you think demand spikes cause blackouts, and you randomize
substations to receive them.

Treat a small share: the grid compensates, nothing fails, no difference
between arms. **No effect.**

Treat a large enough share: the grid fails — and it blacks out the control
substations too, because they are on the same grid. No difference between
arms. **No effect.**

Both readings are wrong, and they are wrong in opposite directions. That
example is most of why this pair got filed, and nothing in it is about social
media.

## Why it belongs in a record about ML practice

The argument concerns randomizing units that interact and asking a question
about the aggregate. That is the shape of every deployed recommender, every
ranking system, every assistant at population scale — and the record's
`analysis-and-evaluation` material is all about models under test, none of it
about systems under deployment. `ADR-052` made room for the second kind eight
hours ago and this is the material that fills it.

Two of the four mechanisms are structural rather than empirical, which is what
makes the account `Active` rather than `Proposed`:

- **SUTVA** is a stated assumption of the design. Assign someone a
  chronological feed and their neighbours still pass them algorithmically
  ranked content. Either the assumption holds or the estimate is not what the
  design claims it is. More data does not help.
- **Hysteresis** is the objection to every withdrawal study, and the
  assumption it breaks is usually invisible until named: that the effect of
  having the thing is the negative of the effect of taking it away. Stop a
  long-time language learner for a week, find fluency intact, conclude the app
  does not teach.

The symmetry is what I was most careful to preserve in the filing. This is
equally not evidence that the effects are real. It bounds what the design can
conclude, in both directions, and the temptation to read it as vindication of
one side is exactly what the record should not do.

## The companion, and why they are filed as one position

The second paper measures the field rather than the phenomenon: 49% of
high-profile social media papers carry an industry tie, mostly undisclosed.

The number that actually reframes it is a different one. **21% of authors.**
Half the output, a fifth of the people. If engagement were broad those figures
would converge; they do not, so a small group with durable relationships is
appearing on half of what gets published — and the same detection finds
undisclosed ties among editors and reviewers, where norms are weakest.

Then the two halves meet. Industry ties are over-abundant in the cluster about
what individual users share, and **sparse in the platform-dynamics cluster** —
precisely the level the methodology paper argues the effects live at. The
incentives point away from the question.

That is a satisfying fit and it is exactly the kind of fit to be careful
about. It is correlational, the authors say so, and **both papers share a
first author**. So the record files it as one position argued twice, and both
consensus notes say so in as many words. Two papers agreeing is worth much
less when one person wrote both.

## One number that is a floor

The 49% is detected from public traces — OpenAlex records, announced RFPs,
fellowships, CVs. Ties that left no trace are missing, so undetected ties push
the figure **down**.

A headline number that errs conservative is unusual enough to be worth saying
out loud, especially after a session in which `DP-010` was filed on four
papers whose most quotable sentence was their least supported one. This is the
opposite shape: the quotable number is the cautious one.

## The obvious next measurement, which nobody has run

The detection method is mechanical and the corpus construction is reusable.
Nothing about it is specific to social media.

This record's own sources are a corpus. Whether the anthology's literature
carries comparable rates of industry connection is unknown, and
`SOTA-tmpxrcae`'s promotion condition asks for exactly that — run somewhere
other than social media, and the most interesting "somewhere" is here.
