Second half of the data-selection gap: attribution — going the other way round
the pipeline, from a model's behaviour back to the data that caused it. The
record held nothing at all. Four papers, two theories, two practices.

## Why this needed four papers and not three

I planned three: the origin (Koh and Liang), the checkpoint-based alternative
(TracIn), and the version that reaches language-model scale (Grosse et al.).
Reading the third turned that into four.

Grosse's limitations section leads — first sentence, before any finding — with
this: influence functions on neural networks are *a poor match to the
counterfactual that motivated them*, and are better understood as
approximating something called the proximal Bregman response function, citing
Bae et al. 2022.

That is the exact shape `CLAUDE.md` describes and `ADR-031` exists for: a
technique everybody uses, published with an explanation that was later
refuted. Filing the three papers without the fourth would have left the
record holding a practice whose stated justification the field had abandoned,
with nothing in the record saying so.

So `LIT-402` went in, and with it the pair:

- `THEORY-017`, `Rejected` — an influence estimate predicts the effect of
  removing a training point and retraining. **Correct where it was validated**,
  which was convex models on MNIST. The `status_note` says that rather than
  implying the original work was careless.
- `THEORY-018`, `Active` — what it estimates on neural networks is the
  PBRF: the effect of removing a point *while keeping predictions close to the
  trained model's*.

Same structure as `THEORY-001`/`THEORY-003` for batch normalization, and
reached the same way — by reading the paper that uses the technique and taking
its first limitation seriously instead of treating it as throat-clearing.

## The thing worth writing down

Bae's decomposition is the most quotable result I have filed this week. The
gap between influence estimates and leave-one-out retraining splits into five
terms. Two are approximation error — linearization, solver. Three are not.

And the three are an order of magnitude *larger* than the two. Which means the
field's reading of that gap — "influence functions are fragile on neural
networks" — had the diagnosis backwards. The estimate was not a bad answer to
the intended question; it was a good answer to a different one, and the three
large terms are exactly the distance between the two questions.

The paper's terminology carries the whole argument: it calls terms 1–3 *gaps*
and terms 4–5 *errors*. A gap means the target moved. An error means the aim
was off. **A method that reliably fails to answer one question is answering a
different one, and the useful move is to find out which.**

## Why the practice survives, and why that had to be checked

`SOTA-246` — find mislabelled training data by self-influence, not by
training loss — is sourced to Koh and Liang, corroborated by TracIn, and
**also sources Bae**, which is unusual and deliberate. Without Bae the practice
rests on `THEORY-017`, which is `Rejected`.

It survives because it never needed the counterfactual. It needs an *ordering*
over training points, and Bae's paper says in as many words that the PBRF
supports the use cases that motivated influence functions, naming mislabelled
examples as one. That sentence is load-bearing, and it is why `LIT-402`
is in `source:` rather than only in the body.

The mechanism is also better than the original paper had. A mislabelled
example is a strong proponent of *itself* — strong because it is an outlier
nothing else supports, a proponent because the model's movement does reduce
its loss with respect to the wrong label it carries. Training loss cannot make
that distinction, because the genuinely hard examples have high loss too.

## The fourth axis inside `data-pipeline`

`SOTA-245` says to state a relation in both orders if you want it usable
in both directions. Grosse's synthetic construction is clean: identical
content, order reversed, and the influence collapses to 0.030 against an
unrelated baseline of 0.020.

Filing it meant deciding it was not `SOTA-239`, which orders the training data
by skill prerequisite. It is not. The `data-pipeline` practices now answer
four questions, and it took filing this one to see the fourth clearly:

- **what to include** — filtering, dedup, pruning
- **in what proportion** — mixing laws, DoReMi
- **in what order the documents arrive** — `SOTA-239`
- **in what order the clauses sit inside a document** — this

I have written the gap between the evidence and the instruction into the
practice rather than smoothing it: **influence is not accuracy.** What was
measured is which sequences a completion is attributable to, not that a model
trained one-directionally fails the reverse question. The inference is natural
and it is still an inference, and the `promote_when` is the behavioural
experiment nobody has run.

## Three recommendations named and not filed

Continuing `ADR-043`'s discipline, since it is only a day old and the test of
it is whether it changes anything.

- Koh and Liang's **R4** (smooth a non-differentiable loss before computing
  influences) is algorithm-local in `ADR-041`'s sense, and this record is not
  where somebody implementing influence functions on a hinge loss will look.
- TracIn's **R3** (retain checkpoints, because attribution needs them and
  cannot reconstruct them) is a real operational instruction with a real cost,
  and the paper *assumes* checkpoints rather than arguing for keeping them.
  Named with its condition: a report where retained checkpoints were what made
  attribution possible, or the reverse.
- Grosse's **R3** (validate a method against the object it estimates, not the
  object it was motivated by) is the best general advice in the whole unit,
  and its only instance here is the paper's own conduct rather than a result.

That last one is the case `ADR-043` is most useful on. It would have been easy
to file — it is obviously right — and the honest condition is hard to write,
which is the signal the decision says to read.
