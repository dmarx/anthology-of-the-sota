The request was to add the phrase-as-lemma paper. Goldberg and Shirtz (2025),
*Language* 101(2) — English syntax, four preregistered surveys, 755
participants, a billion-word corpus, and **not one sentence about a machine**.
Filing it was five minutes. Working out whether it belonged took the rest, and
the useful finding is that the question had no answer here.

## What I expected to find, and did not

I expected a scope rule to consult, and specifically I expected the record to
be a CS-literature collection with a stated edge. It is not either of those.

**Twenty-seven notes carry no arXiv identifier**, and the venues include
*Physical Review Letters*, *Reviews of Modern Physics*, four volumes of *IEEE
Transactions on Automatic Control*, *SIAM*, and a 1963 *Proceedings of the
AMS*. "Outside computer science" was never the boundary; the record crossed it
repeatedly and nobody argued, because each of those is machinery that ML work
runs on.

So the question is not the field. It is what those papers have that this one
does not, and [ADR-032](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-032.md) already named it: **something in the record cites
them.** Nothing here cites Goldberg and Shirtz. On the letter of the only
decision that speaks to admission, this paper is out.

## Why declining it would have been the wrong kind of easy

[DP-009](../../docs/design-principles.md#dp-9) is about exactly this asymmetry and it is worth quoting, because
the principle predicted the shape of the temptation before I met it:

> The first is the cheaper reading, it requires no work, and it is
> *self-confirming*: declining the document leaves the vocabulary looking
> complete, because the evidence that would have shown otherwise has just been
> turned away.

That is precisely what declining would have bought. The record would have gone
on looking like it had a scope, when what it has is a list of things nobody
has yet had a reason to file.

And the principle demands the right kind of argument for declining: "an
argument about the *kind of claim* — not a shrug at the absence of a slot." A
real one is available — the anthology advises on machines, and human
interpretation is not a machine — and it is narrower than it first looks,
which is the whole substance of the pass.

## The thing that made it close

`representation-and-encoding`'s blurb is "how the signal is encoded before the
expensive network sees it — tokenizers and learned latents". **Every practice
under that topic presupposes there is a defensible answer to where a unit of
meaning ends.** None of them measures it; the question is upstream of all of
them and simply assumed.

This paper measures a case where English's answer is "a whole sentence, used
as one word", and where speakers demonstrably act on the word-slot — 77%,
82%, 85% against normed near-paraphrases, on three independent judgements.

The result that makes it more than an anecdote is **Study 4**. The obvious
deflation is that PALs are memorized strings, so the authors re-ran the whole
thing on high-frequency phrases only — `anything goes`, `do it yourself`,
`wait and see` — and every effect reproduced at roughly the same size, with no
frequency dependence anywhere in the main analyses either. **The effect is
carried by the construction, not by the list.** That is what makes it a claim
about structure, and it is the reason the paper is evidence about tokenization
even though it never mentions it.

## What I did, and what I declined to do

`ADR-tmps5go3` proposes three conditions, and the third is the one doing the
work: a note admitted this way must **state what it does not license**. The
failure mode is not the filing. It is a later pass finding an unexplained note
in the reading list and reading a recommendation out of it — which is
`LIT-052` and `LIT-025` again, the two notes whose plausible-looking takeaways
turned out not to be about their papers and which eight practices were built
on. The `note-statuses` vocabulary exists because of those two.

**I did not source a practice from it**, and the temptation was real, because
a practice would make the filing self-justifying. Any practice here would have
been this record's inference wearing Goldberg's citation. [ADR-017](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-017.md) is the
rule against exactly that, and the honest form is a note that says the
recommendation does not exist yet.

**I did not add a topic for it.** [DP-008](../../docs/design-principles.md#dp-8)'s corollary is explicit that a
category added to admit a single document is how a scope stops being one. So
the paper sits under a tag that is the closest available and is not a good
fit, and the ADR says so in its consequences rather than resolving it. One
document is not enough to move a vocabulary.

**The decision is `Proposed` and names what would settle it**: a second paper
from outside the ML literature meeting all three conditions. If none arrives,
this is a single exception and should be relabelled as one rather than left
standing as a rule.

## The part that generalizes

Two passes in two days, and both found the same thing from opposite
directions. Dropout was missing because nothing had ever prompted anyone to
file what everybody agreed on. This paper was nearly missed because nothing
had ever prompted anyone to say what the collection is *about*. In both cases
the record's silence read as a decision and was not one — and in both cases
the evidence was sitting in the record already, as twelve passing mentions
there and twenty-seven non-arXiv notes here.

The query that finds the second kind is cheap and nobody had run it: **list
the venues.** It takes one `grep` and it says more about scope than any
statement of scope in this repository.
