`#137`'s worklist is nearly exhausted, so this pass asked a different
question: not *which papers do the twelve swept bibliographies cite*, but
**which instruments does this record report numbers on**. The answer was not
close.

## The gap was never in the language benchmarks

| instrument | documents | held before this? |
|---|---|---|
| ImageNet (all senses) | 74 | no |
| CIFAR-10/100 | 69 | no |
| MNIST | 26 | no |
| MMLU | 17 | yes |
| LAMBADA | 6 | no |
| MMLU-Pro | 5 | no |

Every benchmark `#137` ranked, and every one this journal proposed as its
tail last pass, sits below MNIST. The three vision datasets are used more
than all of them combined, and the reason none of them showed up in any
previous sweep is that **they are not what this record is about**. They are
what its evidence is measured on — optimizer comparisons, gradient
compression, large-batch scaling, diffusion distillation. A query aimed at
"papers about the topics we file" will never return them, however many
documents depend on them. `DP-004` again, and this is the sharpest instance
yet: the query was not merely narrow, it was aimed at a different axis
entirely.

## Two counting traps, both caught

**`C4` is the fourth row of every claims table.** The first survey put C4 at
138 documents, ahead of ImageNet. `NOTE-*` claims tables number their rows
`C1`, `C2`, `C3`, `C4`, and a plain substring match collects every one of
them. Real count after excluding table rows: 44. The lesson generalises —
short all-caps dataset names collide with this record's own structural
vocabulary, so any benchmark survey wants `\bNAME\b(?!\s*\|)` or a manual
look at the hits.

**`LIT-007` is not the ImageNet paper.** A check for "is ImageNet held?" that
greps literature titles finds *Accurate, Large Minibatch SGD: Training
ImageNet in 1 Hour* and answers yes. It is a paper that uses ImageNet. The
trap is that a dataset's name appears in the titles of the papers that use it
far more often than in the title of the paper that introduced it, so title
matching is biased against exactly the answer you want.

## The three-way ImageNet split, and why it got two documents

"ImageNet" in this record means one of: **ILSVRC**, the 1,000-class challenge
that every top-1 number is on; **ImageNet-64**, a downsampled set scored on
FID, in nine documents; or **TinyImageNet**, in ten. The database Deng et al.
published — millions of images on the WordNet hierarchy — defines no task, no
split and no metric, and is essentially never what a citing document means.

So ImageNet-the-database and ILSVRC-the-challenge are filed as two notes.
That is the third time this shape has come up in a week: ARC-AGI against AI2
ARC, BBH against BIG-bench, now this. In all three the record was reporting
numbers across a split it had no vocabulary for. It is worth noticing that
the failure is not rare and not random — **a successful benchmark acquires a
short name, and the short name then gets reused by its successors and
derivatives**. Any instrument popular enough to be worth filing is, for that
same reason, likely to be ambiguous.

## A citation nothing can check, at the centre of the record

CIFAR is a 2009 University of Toronto technical report: no arXiv id, no DOI,
a URL that already 301s to a different host. `ADR-009` permits a `url` as the
last preference and calls it "a string nothing can check", which was written
about marginal citations. It turns out to describe the record's
second-most-cited instrument. Nothing to do about it — there is no better
identifier to have — but the ADR's framing now has a counter-example worth
knowing about.

## The MNIST finding was better than the note I first wrote

The draft said `THEORY-013` and `SOTA-218` "both turn on a paper that
subsampled MNIST and ImageNet". The lint flagged the citation because
`THEORY-013` is `Rejected`, and chasing that down produced the actual
relationship: `LIT-058` subsampled MNIST and ImageNet to test whether optimal
batch size tracks dataset size, found it "does not depend on data set size in
any consistent way" — and *that experiment is what retired* `THEORY-013`,
while the same experiment is what `SOTA-218` rests on.

Which is the point of the MNIST note. Nothing in this record cites an MNIST
accuracy as evidence a method is good; MNIST is where leave-one-out
retraining and exhaustive subsampling are affordable, and a dataset you can
shrink and retrain is how a scaling claim gets **falsified** rather than
argued about. The lint asking "why are you citing something retired?"
produced a better paragraph than the one it interrupted.
