# LIT-450: How do language models learn facts? Dynamics, curricula and hallucinations
<!-- inactive-ok-file: SOTA-130 — Proposed, and named as one of the task-stage curricula this contrasts with -->
<!-- inactive-ok-file: SOTA-267 — Proposed, and filed in this same contribution from this note -->

## Key takeaways

- **Three phases, not a curve.** A short first phase learning generic
  attribute-value statistics; then a **plateau** at exactly the loss an ideal
  model with no individual-specific knowledge would reach; then the
  acquisition of subject-attribute associations. The plateau's length grows
  with the number of individuals — "almost linearly" in the text, fitted as
  `0.43·N^0.81` (R² = 0.998, 4k–256k individuals, five seeds), which is
  sublinear.
- **The plateau is the recall circuit being built**, and the evidence is an
  intervention rather than a correlation. Patching in the attention patterns
  of a reference checkpoint makes the plateau *disappear*; patterns taken
  progressively later in the plateau are progressively better. Before the
  circuit exists, prediction errors at the attribute token do not backpropagate
  to the name tokens, so the rest of the model cannot learn.
- **Attention patterns from very early training are worse than the untrained
  ones** — the model first attends to attribute-type tokens to predict the
  generic distribution, which is exactly the wrong place for recall.
- **A trade-off in the data distribution.** The paper *argues* (§3.1, "we
  can already develop an intuition") that plateau length is set by the
  frequency of the *most* common individuals and acquisition speed after it
  by the frequency of the *least* common; neither dependence is isolated
  experimentally. What was measured, sampling individuals ∝ `i^(−α)` with
  `α ∈ {0, 0.2, …, 1}` (1 is Zipf): the plateau shortens as `α` rises to an
  optimum of **0.6–0.8 irrespective of population**, and excessive imbalance
  hurts, "likely due to overfitting"; the `α` minimising *final* attribute
  loss grows with the population and as the step budget shrinks (Fig. 4).
  Single seed.
- **A data schedule beats the fixed distributions tried.** What was tested
  is a **warm-up**: train uniformly on a subset of individuals for a few
  epochs, then uniformly on all of them (§3.2, App. B.2). Gains are largest
  when the population is large. A control argues against mere shrinking:
  uniform training on a population of 83k scores 97.72% on it, which is
  63.26% counted over 128k individuals, against 94.92% for the warm-up on
  128k (App. E.2). No skewed distribution annealed toward flat was run,
  the grid optimum for 128k lies at the grid's edge, and it is single-seed.
  The authors call it a promising, "preliminary and task-specific" case of a
  curriculum helping self-supervised learning. *Corrected 2026-09-30. This
  read "start imbalanced to escape the plateau, then flatten … Optimal fixed
  imbalance is an inverse power law with exponent between 1 and 2"; no
  exponent above 1 was tried, and the frequency dependences were presented
  as measured.*
- **Hallucinations arrive with knowledge, not after it.** Overconfident
  predictions on unseen individuals begin "simultaneously" with
  individual-specific knowledge (§4.1) — "shortly after the plateau phase"
  in App. F.2 — though the model is *less* confident on unseen individuals
  than on seen ones, so they "can be detected to some extent" (Fig. M).
- **Fine-tuning is a poor way to add knowledge about *new* individuals**:
  their facts are absorbed slowly and existing parametric memories are
  corrupted within a few hundred steps; replay only partially mitigates it.
  Fine-tuning on rare individuals *seen in pre-training* is the counter-result:
  their accuracy rises from about 50% to close to 70% without the collapse
  (App. F.3, Fig. P). Single seed.

## Standing in the anthology

**It supplies a mechanism for something the record has only ever recorded as
a phenomenon.** Loss plateaus appear throughout the record's training
material as things to wait out or schedule around. This says that at least
one kind of plateau is a circuit under construction, and that the stall is
causal: without the extraction circuit, gradient signal does not reach the
tokens that need it. [THEORY-028](../theory.d/THEORY-028.md) is that account, and the patching
experiment is why it is more than a story.

**It gives the record its first data-schedule practice with a stated
mechanism.** [SOTA-267](../practices.d/SOTA-267.md) is the recommendation. The record holds several
curriculum practices ([SOTA-129](../practices.d/SOTA-129.md), [SOTA-130](../practices.d/SOTA-130.md), [SOTA-123](../practices.d/SOTA-123.md)) and all of them are
about stages of *task* — pretrain, then SFT, then RL. This is a curriculum
over the *distribution within* a stage, justified by two quantities moving in
opposite directions, which is a different and more falsifiable kind of claim.
The two dependences are the paper's argument; what it measured is their
consequences — plateau end and final loss against `α`, population and budget
— and a single-seed warm-up that beat the fixed distributions tried.

**The fine-tuning result belongs beside [SOTA-199](../practices.d/SOTA-199.md)**, which regularises a
narrow fine-tune against the base model's own samples to stop it drifting.
That practice treats drift as a side effect to be contained; this says that
for *knowledge about new entities* specifically the corruption is fast and
the absorption is slow, so the trade is bad before any regulariser is applied.
Fine-tuning on rare entities the model already saw in pre-training did not
show the collapse.

**What it is, and is not.** A synthetic biography task, chosen so that
knowledge can be counted rather than probed. Everything here is a claim about
that setting, and the value is that the setting admits interventions —
attention patching, exact population control — that a natural corpus does
not.
