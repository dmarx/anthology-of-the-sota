# LIT-782: Pick-a-Pic: An Open Dataset of User Preferences for Text-to-Image Generation

Kirstain, Polyak, Singer, Matiana, Penna and Levy, Tel Aviv University and
Stability AI (2023) — [ARXIV-2305.01569](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01569). NeurIPS 2023. Read at v2
(23 Nov 2023), the camera-ready, appendix included. v1 (2 May 2023) was
checked for what changed: it reports PickScore's accuracy as 70.2 from one
run, where v2 gives 70.5 ± 0.142 over three seeds. v2 also adds a footnote
that a second release, Pick-a-Pic v2, holds over a million examples.

## Key takeaways

- **The data comes from people generating for themselves** (§2). Users
  write a prompt, see two images, and pick one or declare a tie. The loser
  is replaced and the round repeats. Two images with ties beat four images,
  and two without ties, on engagement and inter-rater agreement in a pilot.
  The images come from Stable Diffusion 2.1, Dreamlike Photoreal 2.0 (a
  fine-tune of SD 1.5) and SDXL variants, at varied guidance scales. Users
  log in with Gmail or Discord, are capped at 1,000 interactions, and are
  banned for NSFW prompts or judging too fast.
- **The splits are by prompt and by user** (§2). Validation and test take
  500 prompts each, one example per prompt, each prompt from a different
  user. Training gets every example whose prompt is not in them. The
  experiments use an NSFW-filtered snapshot: 583,747 training examples,
  37,523 prompts, 4,375 users. 968,965 rankings had been collected by then.
- **PickScore is a CLIP model trained as a reward model** (§3, Eqs. 1–3).
  The score is CLIP's scaled inner product of text and image embeddings.
  The loss is the KL from the user's choice, [1, 0], [0, 1] or [0.5, 0.5]
  for a tie, to the softmax over the two images' scores. That is the
  InstructGPT reward-model objective with ties added. Each example is
  weighted by the inverse of its prompt's frequency, so heavy prompts do
  not dominate. CLIP-H is fine-tuned for 4,000 steps at learning rate
  3·10⁻⁶ and batch 128, in under an hour on 8 A100s, with no
  hyperparameter search. The checkpoint is chosen by validation accuracy.
  Using the rest of the batch as negatives, as CLIP's own loss does, gives
  65.2 instead (Appendix).
- **It predicts users' choices better than the alternatives** (§4,
  Table 1b). The metric scores a match 1, a tie on one side only 0.5, and
  a miss 0. Each model's tie threshold is tuned on validation. On the test
  set: random 56.8, aesthetics predictor 56.8, CLIP-H 60.8, ImageReward
  61.1, HPS 66.7, expert humans 68.0, PickScore 70.5 ± 0.142.
- **As an evaluation metric it tracks people better than FID or CLIP**
  (§5). Real users' 14,000 preferences on test prompts give Elo ratings for
  45 configurations, four backbones at several guidance scales. Rated by
  each metric instead, Spearman correlation with the users' ratings is
  0.790 ± 0.054 for PickScore, 0.670 for HPS, 0.492 for ImageReward and
  0.313 for CLIP-H (Fig. 7, 50 shuffles of the Elo order). On 100 MS-COCO
  captions and nine configurations (SD 1.5, SD 2.1 and Dreamlike, each at
  guidance 3, 6 and 9), expert rankings correlate 0.917 with PickScore and
  −0.900 with FID (Fig. 6).
- **People prefer higher guidance, and FID penalizes it** (§2 Fig. 3a, §5
  Fig. 5). Users of SDXL-alpha usually prefer images at guidance 9 to those
  at 3. The authors read FID's negative correlation as this: high guidance
  gives vivid images people like and that sit further from MS-COCO's
  photographs. They recommend Pick-a-Pic prompts over MS-COCO captions,
  which describe everyday photographs rather than what users ask for.
- **Best of 100 by PickScore is preferred** (§6, Table 2). For 100 test
  prompts, Dreamlike Photoreal 2.0 generates 100 images each, five seeds by
  twenty prompt templates. Expert annotators prefer PickScore's pick to the
  aesthetics predictor's 85.1% of the time, to CLIP-H's 71.3%, to a random
  image from the untemplated prompt 71.4%, and to a random image overall
  82.0%. PickScore's pick also has the higher aesthetic score 68.5% of the
  time against CLIP-H's pick, and the higher CLIP-H score 90.5% of the time
  against the aesthetic predictor's.

## Where the hedges are

Per [DP-010](../../docs/design-principles.md#dp-10):

- **"Superhuman" is against outsiders.** The expert annotators are "friends
  and colleagues of the authors" (footnote 9). The paper's own point is that
  they do not know what the user meant. So 70.5 against 68.0 says the
  model predicts this user population better than an outsider does. It
  does not say the model judges images better than people.
- **The headroom is small.** Chance under the tie-aware metric is 56.8, so
  PickScore is 13.7 points above it and humans 11.2. The aesthetics
  predictor scores exactly chance.
- **Every agreement with "real users" is in-distribution.** The test set,
  the 14,000 Elo preferences and the best-of-100 prompts all come from
  Pick-a-Pic's own users and prompt distribution, which PickScore was
  trained on. HPS and ImageReward are scored on PickScore's home ground,
  and the paper names data scale, distribution and implementation as
  confounds it did not separate (§7).
- **The FID comparison is nine points.** Three models at three guidance
  scales, 100 captions, ranked by colleague annotators. FID ranks a whole
  configuration and PickScore ranks per image. The correlations are
  between model win ratios, not per-image agreement.
- **It is validated as a judge and a ranker, not as a target.** The
  strongest use shown is picking one of 100 samples. The conclusion
  proposes it for RLHF, and that is how it has since been used. Nothing
  here tests what happens when a model is optimized against it at length.
  Best-of-100 moves only log n − (n − 1)/n, about 3.6 nats, from the base
  sampler. The reward-overoptimization study ([LIT-795](LIT-795.md)) finds that
  true reward under a learned scorer rises and then falls as optimization
  continues, and that RL spends far more KL than best-of-n for the same
  gain. A ranking result does not say where that turn comes for RL.
- **Taste, not correctness.** In disagreements with CLIP-H, PickScore
  "often chooses more aesthetically pleasing images", "at times, at the
  cost of faithfulness to the prompt" (§4, Fig. 4). The dataset also
  contains NSFW prompts and images the filter missed, which the authors
  acknowledge (§2, §8).
- **One run of everything but PickScore's training.** The ranking and
  evaluation studies are single runs. The three seeds are for the
  scoring function only.

## Which comparisons are like for like

- **PickScore against CLIP-H, the aesthetics predictor and humans (Table
  1b)** share the test set, the metric and per-model tie thresholds. Humans
  choose ties directly rather than through a tuned threshold.
- **HPS and ImageReward** are other groups' models trained on other data,
  evaluated on this paper's test set. Not like for like, and the paper
  says why.
- **PickScore against FID (Fig. 6)** compares a per-image, prompt-aware
  score with a set-level, prompt-blind distance. The setting is MS-COCO,
  which the authors chose as "convenient" for FID.
- **Best-of-100 (Table 2)** compares selectors on the same 100 images per
  prompt. The baselines include random selection, so part of the margin is
  just that any selector beats chance.

## Standing in the anthology

It extends CLIP ([LIT-588](LIT-588.md)). PickScore keeps CLIP's two-tower architecture
and its scaled inner-product score (Eq. 1) and starts from the OpenCLIP
CLIP-H checkpoint. What it changes is the training signal: one image of a
pair preferred over the other, the reward-model objective of InstructGPT
([LIT-377](LIT-377.md)), instead of matching captions to images. Its zero-shot CLIP-H
baseline is the starting checkpoint. Fine-tuning on user preferences takes
it from 60.8 to 70.5, and CLIP-H ranks the 45 configurations at 0.313
against PickScore's 0.790.

It measured itself against FID ([LIT-611](LIT-611.md)) as a way to rank text-to-image
models, and found FID anti-correlated with expert preference across nine
configurations. The reason it gives is classifier-free guidance
([LIT-693](LIT-693.md)): higher guidance is preferred and moves samples away from
MS-COCO's statistics. That is the trade [SOTA-424](../practices.d/SOTA-424.md) says to choose
deliberately, measured here on human preference rather than on IS.

**Two later papers in the record depend on it, in different ways.**
Diffusion-DPO ([LIT-774](LIT-774.md)) trains offline on the Pick-a-Pic v2 pairs,
851,293 of them. It picks checkpoints and sweeps β by median PickScore on
500 validation prompts, and confirms its headline with crowdworkers. Its
own description of the data, images "generated by SDXL-beta and
Dreamlike", leaves out the SD 2.1 images this paper lists for the first
release. Flow-GRPO ([LIT-779](LIT-779.md)) uses PickScore as the reward itself and
reports it rising from 21.72 to 23.31. With the KL term removed, its
outputs collapse to one style while PickScore and the other reward models
stay high. That is the use this paper's evidence covers least.
[SOTA-442](../practices.d/SOTA-442.md)'s evidence on that task is PickScore-judged as well.
