# ADR-003: Apply the topic consolidation and let the config enforce one primary topic

## Context

`docs/readme_llm/sections/registry_naming_conventions.md.j2` specified a
consolidation of the topic vocabulary from 22 categories down to 7, complete
with scope definitions, naming rules (hyphenated-lowercase, gerunds for
process topics), a migration procedure, and maintenance rules for adding,
merging, splitting and deprecating categories. It also stated the rule that
matters most: **each recommendation is tagged with exactly one primary
topic**, plus optional secondaries.

It was a decision record in every respect except the ones that make a
decision record useful. It had no number, so nothing could cite it. It lived
in a Jinja template, so it rendered into generated documentation as
description rather than sitting anywhere as a rule. And it was never applied:
at migration time `registry.yaml` still carried the original 22 topics, and
`research.yaml` had accumulated 172 distinct topic strings, most used once.

172 strings across 118 papers is not a taxonomy. It includes `efficiency`
(29 uses) alongside `memory efficiency`, `memory-efficiency` and
`memory-optimization`; `inference optimization` alongside
`inference-optimization`; and `llama2`, which is a model name.

## Decision

Apply it, and put each piece where something can check it.

The seven categories are the ones the 2024 document specified, unchanged:
`training-optimization`, `systems-optimization`, `model-stability`,
`distributed-optimization`, `data-pipeline`, `attention-techniques`,
`model-architecture`. They live in `record/practices.d/tags.yaml` with the
scopes transcribed from that document.

The one-primary-topic rule becomes a `tag_groups` constraint in `luria.toml`
with `require = "exactly-one"`. Secondary tags outside the group stay
unconstrained, as specified.

The `LIT` scheme gets those seven plus five more —
`generative-modeling`, `vision-and-graphics`, `adaptation-and-tuning`,
`inference-optimization`, `analysis-and-evaluation` — because the reading
list covers ground the practice registry does not. Roughly a third of the
corpus is generative, vision or theory work that never produced a training
recommendation, and forcing it into the seven would have meant either a
wrong tag or a category that means nothing.

The mapping from all 172 original strings onto the vocabulary is committed as
data at `src/scripts/migration/topic_map.yaml`, with the seven papers where
automated scoring read the paper wrong listed individually with reasons. The
original strings survive on every note in a free-form `keywords:` field, so
the consolidation stays reviewable and nothing is destroyed.

## Alternatives considered

- **Keep the 172 strings as tags.** Honest about what the corpus says, and it
  produces 172 generated tag pages, most with one entry. A browsing axis with
  one item per value is not a browsing axis.
- **Consolidate but leave the rule in prose.** What was tried. The document
  is well-written and specific and had no effect for two years, which is the
  evidence.
- **Use the seven for both schemes.** Simpler to explain, one vocabulary to
  learn. It puts a diffusion sampler paper under `training-optimization` or
  `model-architecture` and makes both pages worse.
- **Derive secondary tags during the import.** Tried, and reverted: the
  generic strings map somewhere, so Adam came out tagged `model-architecture`
  because `neural-networks` was in its topic list. A secondary tag is an
  editorial judgement the import cannot make.

## Consequences

Adding a category is now a config change plus a `tags.yaml` entry, and the
lint refuses a practice that carries none of the seven or more than one. That
is the intended cost: the vocabulary can no longer drift by accretion, which
is exactly how it reached 172.

The maintenance rules from the 2024 document — merge above 50% scope overlap,
split when a category becomes too broad — are not enforceable and are not
claimed to be. They stay as guidance, in this decision, where they can at
least be cited.
