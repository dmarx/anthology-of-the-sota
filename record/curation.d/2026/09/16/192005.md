I asked luria for two features — a vocabulary that can say what it is
([LU-#279](https://github.com/dmarx/luria/issues/279)) and one that can say something when its own rule fires ([LU-#273](https://github.com/dmarx/luria/issues/273)) —
and then used them on one table out of nine, because `topics` was the one I
had a complaint about.

Coming back to do the other eight, the striking thing is that **I did not have
to write the prose.** It was already there, in YAML comments directly above
each table, and in some cases it was the best writing in the file:

> A paper's status is about its standing in the anthology, not about whether
> its findings are true. Adam is `Active` here because it is worth reading;
> whether any particular thing it recommends is still what you should do is
> the SOTA scheme's business, and the two can disagree.

That is the distinction the whole two-scheme split exists for. It has been
sitting where no reader could reach it, and — the sharper point — where no
*violation* could quote it. Somebody writing `status: Deprecated` on a LIT
note is exactly the person that paragraph was written for, and the one person
guaranteed not to be reading `luria.yaml` at that moment.

## What moved and what stayed

The cut is between two kinds of sentence, and it turned out to be clean:

- **What this set is** → `blurb`. Renders on every value page and heads the
  index.
- **What to do when you cannot find your word in it** → `alert`. Prints under
  the violation.
- **How this file came to look like this** → stays a comment. The `inert-status`
  history behind the five practice statuses, the two mis-attributed readings
  that produced `note-statuses` — those are facts about this project's past,
  not about the vocabulary, and a reader hitting a bad status does not need
  them.

Seven of the nine got an alert. `adr-tags` and `dp-tags` did not, because the
fields naming them are open, and an alert on an open vocabulary never fires —
which is the loose end luria's own `ADR-110` names and does not close.

## The three that will earn their keep

`note-statuses`: *the value you want may be no note at all.* The record's
convention is that an absent NOTE means the paper is unread, and `Unread` is
reserved for the deliberate set-aside. Nobody would guess that from a list of
four words.

`consensus`: *`unassessed` is a real answer rather than a gap.* Most of the
corpus carries it, honestly, and the failure mode is somebody inventing a
sixth word to avoid admitting they have not looked.

`lit-statuses`: *if you mean the advice has moved on, that is a status on the
practice.* This is [ADR-002](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-002.md) restated at the moment somebody is about to
violate it.

## A mistake worth keeping

I wrote the conversion as a script — find each table, insert the set keys,
indent the values under `terms:` — and checked it by asserting that every
vocabulary's values survived unchanged. They did. All nine reported `ok`.

The script had also indented `issue_url:` and `paths:`, the two top-level keys
that happen to sit between the last vocabulary and `schemes:`, straight into
the `vocabularies:` block. `luria link --fix` crashed on the next command.

My check asked *did the values change?* and could not have answered *did
anything else?* — a filter and its complement, which is [DP-004](../design-principles.md#dp-4) exactly, in a
pass I wrote twenty minutes after quoting [DP-004](../design-principles.md#dp-4) in a curation entry about a
different pass. The fixed version compares the whole parsed document and
asserts the top-level key list is unchanged. That check costs three lines and
would have failed loudly on the first run.

The lesson I keep re-learning in a new costume: a green assertion is evidence
about the assertion.
