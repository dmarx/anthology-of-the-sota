The most-revisited absent paper in the feed was Bergsma et al.'s *Power
Lines*, at 4,440 seconds across seven days. It turned out to be the follow-up
to the practice I filed the previous session — and to carry, alongside it, the
retirement of one of the record's oldest recommendations.

## What I went looking for

`SOTA-255`, filed yesterday, says to raise weight decay under data constraint,
and its conditions say plainly what it could not supply: *"the paper gives no
way to predict the optimum from the parameter-to-token ratio, so the practice
is really 'sweep it, and sweep upward'."*

Power Lines is a power law in exactly that ratio. The obvious move was to
file it as the missing rule and promote.

## Why I did not

The two are fitted in disjoint regimes and nobody has joined them.

Power Lines normalizes its timescale by the step count on the explicit ground
that *"LLM pre-training only uses one epoch of data"*, and fits from 20 to
1280 tokens-per-parameter. `SOTA-255`'s source epochs a fixed corpus many
times at parameter-to-token ratios *above* Chinchilla — tokens-per-parameter
below 20 and often below 1. Opposite ends of the same axis, different
assumptions about repetition, and no published check that either
extrapolates into the other's range.

So the record now holds two well-measured answers to one question and an
unexamined gap between them, and both documents say so. That is a better
state than a composition I would have had to invent.

## The thing I was not looking for

`SOTA-097` — *optimal batch size scales as `C^0.24`* — has been in the record
since its first month, sourced to Kaplan's equation 1.7. Its own body
contained this sentence: *"That the batch exponent has not been re-derived by
anyone since is a reason to hold it loosely, not a reason to trust it."*

It has now been re-derived twice. The variable is wrong: optimal and critical
batch size scale in the **token budget**, not in compute. Zhang et al. ran
the decoupling experiment — hold `N` fixed, hold `D` fixed, see which one
moves the answer — and got `D^0.462`. Bergsma et al., from a different
architecture, corpus, context length, parameterization, schedule and tuning
strategy, got `D^0.47`. Neither ran the other's protocol.

`SOTA-062` — *scale batch size with model size, sub-linearly* — falls to the
same experiment, and its body had invited it in the same words: *"a reader
with a measurement should prefer it."*

## The part worth keeping

**Kaplan's fit is not an arithmetic error, and writing it up as one would
have been the easy story.** Along the Chinchilla line `N` and `D` scale
together, so `C ≈ 6ND` moves with `D` and a power law in either describes the
data equally well. Equation 1.7 measured a projection and reported it as the
thing. The tell is only visible with runs *off* that line: plotted against
compute, points at equal `D` fall on parallel lines rather than one.

That is the same failure mode Chinchilla found in Kaplan's allocation
exponent, in a different coordinate — which is why `LIT-028` stays `Active`
with a `corrected_by` rather than being retired. A method that generalizes
past the fits its authors could check is not discredited by the fits it could
not.

**And the two hyperparameter papers are making the same argument about
different constants.** `beta_2 = 0.95` is a decay per optimizer step, and an
optimizer step is not a fixed quantity of data — shrink the batch 512× and
the averaging window in tokens shrinks with it, which is the entire content
of "small batches are unstable". `lambda = 0.1` means nothing without the
learning rate and step count it multiplies. Both papers name the implicit
denominator and the constant becomes transferable. I did not expect the
cluster to have a thesis.

## Three places I had to be careful

**The retags.** `SOTA-062` and `SOTA-061` were both filed under
`model-architecture`, and the correction edge from the new practice could not
bind until they moved. That is exactly the shape `ADR-035` warns against —
retagging to make a relation expressible. They are also, plainly,
mis-filed: `batch size` is named in the `training-optimization` blurb and
nothing in the `model-architecture` blurb covers it. I retagged them, said in
both history entries that the retag is right on its own terms and that it
also happens to bind the edge, and cited `ADR-035` at the site so the next
reader can check the reasoning rather than the outcome.

**Superseding a well-cited practice is not free.** `SOTA-097` was cited 15
times across 13 files and `SOTA-062` 10 times across 9. Every one of those
became an unacknowledged citation of a retired document the moment the status
changed, which is the lint doing its job. Eight were in documents I did not
write; I read each site before vouching for it, and two of them —
`SOTA-198`'s section on the scaling-law exponent, and `LIT-028`'s standing —
needed the prose changed rather than a directive, because both would now
mislead a reader.

**`SOTA-198` came out better, not worse.** It says to measure the gradient
noise scale rather than predict the batch from compute, and it recorded the
disagreement with `SOTA-097` as informative-and-open. The disagreement is now
resolved *in its favour*: a noise scale rising through a run is what "critical
batch size grows with tokens seen" looks like from inside the run. The
temptation with a supersession is to treat everything adjacent as damaged.
Here the adjacent practice was right and its reason improved.

## On the ranking, again

Two of the top three by revisit count remain unfilable — political exposure
bias on Twitter, the work capacity of channels with memory. The feed keeps
ordering them first, and they keep not belonging. That is not a defect in the
ranking; a revisit count measures attention, and this record measures whether
a thirteen-topic vocabulary can carry the claim.
