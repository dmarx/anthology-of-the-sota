# LIT-053: High Performance I/O For Large Scale Deep Learning

Aizman, Maltby and Breuel, NVIDIA (2020) — [ARXIV-2001.01858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.01858)

## Key takeaways

- The premise is a mismatch, not a performance bug: distributed filesystems
  were designed for access patterns and usability requirements that deep
  learning jobs do not have. Petascale training reads enormous numbers of
  small objects in shuffled order, which is close to the worst case for
  systems tuned around directory trees and random access to files.
- WebDataset is the format answer — training data as *sharded tar archives*,
  a standards-based container needing no special reader, so the job's access
  pattern becomes sequential streaming of large files. Shuffling happens
  within and across shards rather than by seeking.
- AIStore is the storage answer: a scalable, easy-to-deploy backend for the
  same workload.
- Evaluated on image classification across local SSDs, single-node NFS, and
  two identical bare-metal clusters running HDFS and AIStore — the point of
  the comparison being that the format's benefit is not tied to one backend.

## Standing in the anthology

The source of [SOTA-077](../practices.d/SOTA-077.md) (tar archives for dataset storage) and of the
buffering, prefetch and worker-count practices that follow from streaming
shards ([SOTA-078](../practices.d/SOTA-078.md), [SOTA-079](../practices.d/SOTA-079.md), [SOTA-080](../practices.d/SOTA-080.md)). Those numbers — a shuffle buffer of two to
three batches, four workers per GPU — are operational rules of thumb rather
than results this paper reports, and they should be read that way; what the
paper establishes is the format and why sequential access is the thing worth
buying.

The companion to [LIT-050](LIT-050.md): that paper measures the stall, this one changes
the storage layout that causes it.
