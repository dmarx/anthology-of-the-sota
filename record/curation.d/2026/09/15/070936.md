---
title: 'Two notes that said, in their own words, that they had nowhere to go'
created: '2026-09-15T07:09:36'
tags:
- record
---

**The `THEORY` scheme came out of a survey that was looking for something
else.** The question on the table was whether the practice registry
accommodates knowledge that is strictly theoretical, or whether that needs a
scheme of its own. The way to answer it was to look at what the record was
already doing with such knowledge, and the answer turned out to be written
down in two places, by earlier contributions, in plain language.

The first pass was a regex over practice titles for verb phrases that
describe rather than instruct. It returned seventeen, which was too many:
"Employ mixed precision to reduce memory usage" matched on *reduce* and is an
instruction by any reading. Reading the seventeen by hand left eight that
genuinely state a behaviour — `SOTA-010`, `SOTA-012`, `SOTA-037`, `SOTA-038`,
`SOTA-040`, `SOTA-064`, `SOTA-092`, `SOTA-097`. The wrong theory worth
recording is that the regex count was ever the evidence. Eight of 208 is not
a large number, and on its own it would have argued for leaving things alone.

The evidence that mattered came from the other side. Scanning for `LIT`
notes that no practice cites returns 48 of 225 — and in
`analysis-and-evaluation`, which is the topic whose blurb explicitly says
"theory, interpretability and debugging belong here too", it is 6 of 12.
Half the topic is papers the registry cannot reach.

Then two of those notes turned out to have anticipated the whole question.
`LIT-019` and `LIT-039` each carry a section headed **"Carries no practice,
deliberately"**, and `LIT-019`'s says exactly what the problem is: the
lottery-ticket procedure "costs a full dense training run before it produces
anything, so as an instruction it reads *train the model, then train a
smaller one* — which is a finding about neural networks rather than advice
about how to train one." Someone had already worked out that the document
was not a practice, had had nowhere else to file it, and had written the
reasoning into the note as a defence of an anomaly.

`LIT-223` is the third instance and the sharpest. It was filed to correct a
conflation, it says in its own body that "the practices that turn on the
*mechanism* — why BN permits what it permits — belong here", and nothing in
the record cites it. Nothing could. What that paper establishes is that batch
normalization does not work for the reason its title gives, and there is no
instruction in that: nobody's behaviour changes. Three statuses are needed to
say it — the practice `Active`, the paper `Active`, the explanation
`Rejected` — and there were two schemes to put them in.

That is the finding: the record was not silent about theory, it was
**annotating the workaround**. The notes that had nowhere to go said so, at
length, in prose that a scheme would have made unnecessary.

The scheme was built around the batch-normalization pair rather than around
the eight practices, and deliberately so. Re-filing an existing practice
leaves a `SOTA` code that is neither `Rejected` nor `Superseded` but simply
moved, and this record's rule is to retire by changing status and never by
deleting — a rule that has nothing to say about a document that was filed in
the wrong drawer. That needs its own decision, and bundling it into the one
that creates the drawer would have hidden the question.

One thing checked and worth not re-checking: `luria` does support a crossing
converse, so `THEORY.explains` → `SOTA.explained_by` is a declared pair and
`luria link --fix` writes both ends. `luria.yaml` claimed otherwise in a
comment on `contested_by:`, which had been true when it was written and
stopped being true at `LU-ADR-097`. The field still has no converse; the
comment now says that is a choice about what a reading note should hold,
rather than a limit.
