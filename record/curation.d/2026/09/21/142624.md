`SOTA-240` says: apply dropout where the model can memorize what it is shown,
and not where it cannot. `Active`, `converged`, filed on Srivastava's sweet
spot and a 2026 recipe that reaches for dropout under exactly the predicted
condition.

A reader on an underfitting model follows that and concludes: no dropout.

The correct conclusion is: no dropout *after the first stretch*.

## What separates the two uses

Not the rate. Not the placement in the network. The **schedule**, and one
measurement tells them apart.

ViT-T on ImageNet-1K, training loss:

- baseline **3.443**
- standard dropout **3.885**
- early dropout **3.394**

A regularizer raises training loss. That is what regularizing *is* — you give
up fit to buy generalization, and the 3.885 is dropout doing its job on a
model that did not need it, costing six points of top-1 in the process.

The 3.394 is not that. Nothing about a regularizer lowers training loss, so
whatever early dropout is, it is not regularization. It is an optimization
intervention wearing the same code path.

That is why the practice filed here says: **if you try this and training loss
does not fall, you have not reproduced the effect**, whatever the accuracy
did. Accuracy is the number people report and it cannot distinguish the two
operators. Training loss can, and does, at a glance.

## The contradiction that makes the mechanism credible

Section 3 opens with something that should not happen. The dropout model
produces **smaller** gradients and travels **farther** from its
initialization.

Their image is two walkers, one with long strides and one with short. The
short-strided one gets farther because it keeps going the same way.

Then they measure it, and the second measurement is the one that counts.
Pairwise angular spread among mini-batch gradients falls with dropout — but
that alone is consistent with consistently going the *wrong* way. So they also
compute the gradient over the entire training set, with dropout in inference
mode, and measure the angle to it. That falls too, early, and rises after
about a thousand iterations.

The bias–variance statement is exact and is the whole account. Without
dropout, a mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full-dataset
gradient: same network, random subset. With dropout it is biased — each batch
runs through a different sub-network. Variance falls by more than bias costs,
so the error falls. Early, when the model is moving fast, variance dominates
and the trade pays. Later there is no variance left to remove and only the
bias remains. The curves cross, and dropout goes back to being a regularizer.

One account, two regimes, and the crossing is the boundary. That is a much
better shape than "dropout also helps underfitting, somehow".

## What kept the theory at `Proposed`

The variance and error reduction is replicated: five model/optimizer pairs,
same sign, AdamW through plain SGD, ViT through ConvNeXt.

The **crossing point** is one model. "About 1000 iterations" is where ViT-T's
curves meet, and nothing predicts where it sits elsewhere.

That matters more than it looks, because the crossing is the only part of the
account that would be *useful*. If it predicts the right switch point, the
metric is a tool you can apply to a new setting. If it does not, then the
practice's robustness result — any switch point from 1% to 50% of total epochs
works — is doing all the work, and the crossing is a story told about a number
that turned out not to matter.

The paper reports both facts and declines to connect them, which is the right
thing to have done and is exactly why the account is offered rather than
established.

## And they reported their error bars

Three seeds, average standard deviation 0.142%. So +0.4 through +0.9 is real
and ConvNeXt-F's +0.2 is not, and a reader can tell which is which without
guessing.

That is worth noting out loud one contribution after the quantized-ES paper,
where the only variance estimate available was inferred from an approximation
beating the oracle it approximates. The difference is three numbers in an
appendix.
