Two days of citation repair ended with a question worth more than the
repair: why did a record built specifically to make evidence structural sit
for two years with a quarter of its practices resting on papers that do not
exist?

The answer is not that nobody looked. It is that **the schema had no way to
express the difference between a well-supported practice and an unsupported
one**, so looking would not have helped.

## The cardinality was the camouflage

`source:` held exactly one code. That made every practice look identical in
the only dimension that matters for judging evidence: each had one source,
so each was maximally sourced.

The 39 practices that turned out to rest on synthetic notes were therefore
*indistinguishable from the best-supported practices in the registry*.
[SOTA-121](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-121.md) (use Muon) has scaling-law evidence, a 1T-parameter production run
and independent adoption at 90M. [SOTA-044](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-044.md) ("pin memory for CPU-GPU
transfers") had a fabricated citation. Both had one source. Both passed the
lint. Nothing could ask the question that separates them.

Meanwhile the practices with the *most* evidence were the ones the field
served worst. [SOTA-132](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-132.md)'s body names seven notes; its `source:` named one. So
the structured field was not merely uninformative for the strongest entries
— it was actively misleading, reporting one paper where seven exist. That is
[DP-002](../design-principles.md#dp-2)'s failure mode with a twist: not a field that says nothing, but a
field that says something false with confidence.

[ADR-010](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-010.md) makes it a list. The immediate payoff is a query that could not be
written before:

    Proposed practices, by source count:
      [SOTA-122](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-122.md)  1   [SOTA-124](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-124.md)  1   [SOTA-125](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-125.md)  1   [SOTA-130](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-130.md)  1
      [SOTA-133](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-133.md)  1   [SOTA-136](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-136.md)  1   [SOTA-141](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-141.md)  1   [SOTA-144](../../record/practices.d/SOTA-144.md)  1

All eight sit at exactly one. That is the record obeying a rule it had
written down in prose and could not enforce — [LIT-121](../../record/literature.d/LIT-121.md) and [LIT-134](../../record/literature.d/LIT-134.md) each say a
practice "stays Proposed until a result from outside the authors' group is
in the record". It was true, and until now, unverifiable.

## The guard was fired before it was trusted

Following luria's rule that a new check runs once on a real case first,
three deliberate breakages, all caught:

- `source: LIT-999` → *resolves to no LIT document*
- `source: ADR-001` → *is not a LIT code — a SOTA document's `source` names
  a LIT document*
- a valid first element with a broken second → *LIT-998 resolves to no
  document*

The third is the one that matters, and is the reason `many = true` is not
cosmetic. Without it luria stringifies a list and checks only its first
element, so a practice could name one real note and three imaginary ones and
pass. That is the exact failure this record just spent two days repairing,
and it would have been reintroduced by a plural `source:` declared carelessly.

## What was rejected, and why it was tempting

`source:` plus a separate `corroborated_by:` reads better and keeps the
primary unambiguous. It was rejected because it asks the filer to grade
evidence at the moment they are least able to: whether a second paper
corroborates a practice or merely touches it usually becomes clear only when
a third arrives. A flat list with a meaningful first element records the one
thing reliably known at filing time — which paper this came from — and defers
the grading. The graded version can be layered on; ungrading would mean
re-litigating every entry.

## The collision, and the thing luria already had

Two branches opened ninety minutes apart both minted [LIT-144](../../record/literature.d/LIT-144.md) and [LIT-145](../../record/literature.d/LIT-145.md) for
different papers. Nothing would have caught it: each branch was internally
consistent, `luria lint` had no reason to look, and git merges two files with
different names and no conflict. It was caught because one session read the
other's pull request.

luria has had `allocate = "merge"` and temporary codes for exactly this the
whole time, and this record had never turned it on — the default is
filing-time allocation, and the default had been fine while there was one
contributor. [ADR-013](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-013.md) turns it on for all four schemes.

The lesson generalizes past numbering: **the record adopted a tool and then
used the parts of it that resembled what it did before.** Field groups,
reference typing with `many`, merge allocation, content pins — all of it was
sitting in the configuration surface unused, and two of the four failures
this week were things luria could already prevent. The next review should
probably read `luria --help` against the record rather than the other way
round.

## Left open

[ADR-011](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-011.md) (lineage as a field) and [ADR-012](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-012.md) (practice altitude) are filed as
Proposed rather than adopted, because both need something before they are
safe: [ADR-011](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-011.md) needs a chain view upstream, since a relation that is declared
but never rendered looks maintained while rotting, and [ADR-012](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-012.md) needs its
three-value vocabulary tried on the practices whose filers are still
reachable before it is imposed on the inherited 119.
