**`SOTA-291` went `Proposed` → `Active` ([#180](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/issues/180)).** Its condition read: *a
specific-affordance evaluation run at collective scale with the group-level
outcome actually measured — the source notes two large platform studies that
tested realistic ranking changes and assessed only individual outcomes, so the
missing piece is small and nameable. A further argument that individual trials
are inadequate is not it; that is the premise.*

That is a condition written to refuse the easy satisfier, and the Change.org
paper meets it on every clause: one affordance (a drafting tool in one writing
flow), a coherent counterfactual (three countries got it eleven weeks before a
fourth), a long enough window against a 65-week pre-period, and outcomes
measured **at the platform level** — the share of petitions clearing a
signature threshold, site-wide homogeneity, total participation.

The part that makes it a genuine second source rather than a follow-on is that
the authors **arrive at the design from the practice's own reasoning, without
the practice**. Their limitations section: *"As online platforms involve
deeply interconnected networks of users, causal estimates of interventions on
individual-level outcomes may suffer from bias and require internal platform
access. Instead, we estimate here the causal impact of the intervention to the
system."* That is `THEORY-048` restated by people who were not arguing for it —
they were trying to get an estimate they could defend, and interference pushed
them to the same place. Two groups reaching a method independently is worth
more than one group being cited.

Held at `emerging` rather than `converged` for a reason worth stating: one
worked example proves the design is *available*, not that the field runs it.
And the two documents arguing for the programme still share a first author, so
the consensus note keeps saying so.

**The real find was in an appendix.** The abstract says AI access changed
lexical features and homogeneity but not outcomes. True, and not the
interesting part. The interesting part is that the three lexical features were
chosen *because* they predicted petition success before the tool existed; the
tool moved all three toward the successful profile; **independent human raters
confirmed the text genuinely improved** — quality d = 0.96, persuasiveness
d = 0.86; outcomes went flat-to-down; and then, reported once in an appendix
and once in a discussion sentence, the predictive strength of those features
and of rated quality **weakened or reversed**.

That inversion is what turns a fact about petitions into a lesson. A feature
that *causes* an outcome does not stop predicting it when more people have it.
A feature that *distinguishes* does. So the association everyone would have
used to steer the deployment was partly a fact about the population, and the
deployment changed the population. The homogeneity number — mean pairwise
similarity up 23% — is what that looks like from outside.

`DP-010` again, and this is the fourth instance this session: the sentence
carrying the most weight is the one given the least space. It is also the one
nobody preregistered.

**Why the theory is `Proposed` and the practice is not contingent on it.** The
authors offer three mechanisms and test none: thin substance, reader suspicion
of AI-styled text, and diminished author ownership reducing the off-platform
promotion that actually drives petition success. Two of the three predict the
same null, and the suspicion account predicts the *reversal* too — a feature
that becomes a marker of AI use acquires a negative coefficient for reasons
that have nothing to do with scarcity. So `THEORY-051` carries a
promotion condition asking for predictive validity tracked against the
feature's **dispersion**, which is the measurement that separates dilution from
re-interpretation.

`SOTA-298` deliberately does not rest on any of the three: under all of
them the proxy stopped tracking the outcome and only the outcome would have
told you, and that is the whole recommendation.

**Something I checked before writing it down.** The paper's prose says the
decline in repeat writers' second petitions "was greater when users had AI
access for their second petition". The odds ratios are Pre/Pre 0.825 (not
significant), Pre/Post 0.754, Post/Post **0.662**. The largest decline is the
cohort that had access for *both* petitions, so gaining access is not the
variable; the Pre/Pre interval [0.648, 1.050] contains both other point
estimates; and the authors name regression to the mean themselves. The honest
reading is a consistency check that failed to contradict the platform-level
result, which is worth something and is not the individual-level confirmation
it reads as. Both the note and the practice say so.

**`SOTA-292` used rather than cited.** The industry-ties check this record
filed a day ago got run against this paper, which is the point of having it:
academic authors, publicly accessible data only, launch dates confirmed with
Change.org staff, and a conclusion distinctly unflattering to the platform.
Clean. A practice that only ever gets cited and never applied is a practice
nobody has tested the cost of.

**And an unplanned convergence.** `SOTA-172` and `SOTA-295` — both filed or
touched earlier today — are about generated corpora collapsing toward
themselves, diagnosed by n-gram over-concentration. This is the same collapse
measured by embedding similarity in text that **people wrote**, holding a tool.
Two different causal paths to the same statistical place, one where the model
generates and one where the model suggests. Nobody has written the document
that says whether they are the same phenomenon, and that is a better question
than anything I could have picked off the worklist deliberately.
